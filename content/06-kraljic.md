# Kraljic: choose the right supply strategy

The sample ten-point question asks you to **describe, exemplify and discuss** the Kraljic matrix. You need more than four names. Explain the axes, place all four categories correctly, give examples and suitable strategies, then discuss context, power, change and limitations. Kraljic's 1983 article also contains a wider four-stage process beyond the familiar matrix.

The purpose is to move purchasing from a uniform clerical approach toward differentiated supply management. The right approach to office stationery is unlikely to be the right approach to a unique engine essential to production. Both can be “purchases”, but their managerial consequences differ.

## Two dimensions

**Profit impact** concerns the purchase's importance to business results. Spending volume can matter, but so can effects on quality, growth, performance and the value of the final product. **Supply risk** concerns difficulty or uncertainty in obtaining the requirement, including supplier availability, substitution, technology, market conditions and logistics.

In the lecture, profit impact increases upward and supply risk increases to the right. Some reproductions rotate axes, so always label them. In this guide, high-impact/low-risk items appear at the top left and high-impact/high-risk items at the top right.

These are contextual examples. A flavour can be critical and difficult to replace in one food product but readily available in another. The matrix classifies a purchase situation, not the eternal nature of an object. A standard raw material may become risky during disruption.

## Routine or non-critical purchases

Routine items have relatively low profit impact and low supply risk. The main opportunity is often to reduce transaction effort. A manager should not spend three hours negotiating a tiny saving on a widely available stationery order if the administrative work costs more than the saving.

Rationalisation can include standardisation, simple ordering arrangements, consolidated orders, catalogues and efficient processing. The objective is a reliable, low-effort way to meet the need. It is not permission to ignore the requirement entirely; missing a routine item can still cause inconvenience.

At Campus Tees, ordinary pens or standard office paper are plausible routine purchases. A simple approved catalogue may be enough. The key exam explanation is the fit between low strategic significance, available supply and administrative efficiency.

## Leverage purchases

Leverage items have high profit impact but relatively low supply risk. There are enough credible alternatives for the buyer to use competition and purchasing volume. Competitive bidding, supplier comparison, volume concentration and negotiation can create gains.

For Campus Tees, standard blank shirts bought in large quantities might be leverage items if several qualified suppliers can reliably provide equivalent quality. Their spending impact is meaningful, while switching does not create an unacceptable supply problem. The buyer can compare total offers and aggregate volume.

“Low risk” must be justified. Two suppliers may both depend on the same upstream factory or material. A nominal alternative may not meet the required printability or colour consistency. The matrix is only as good as the assessment of real availability and substitutability.

Using leverage also does not mean pushing suppliers to the point that quality or future capacity collapses. A narrow short-term saving can damage the overall supply system. Kraljic's broader process considers the relative strength of the buyer and the supply market rather than assuming unlimited buyer power.

## Bottleneck purchases

Bottleneck items have relatively low profit impact but high supply risk. They may represent little spending yet be difficult to obtain. The managerial priority is supply continuity. Safety stock, assurance arrangements, alternative qualification and redesign can be more useful than chasing a small unit-price reduction.

Imagine a low-cost replacement connector needed for Campus Tees' printer. It accounts for little annual spending, but only one supplier currently provides the compatible component. A missing connector can stop printing. The small purchase price does not mean that supply failure is harmless.

The lecture gives flavours as its example and safety stock as the suggested strategy. Explain why that might make sense: a specialised flavour may have few sources, and a buffer can protect production while supply is uncertain. Then qualify the response: shelf life, storage cost and the feasibility of alternatives still matter.

Do not confuse bottleneck items with strategic items simply because both have high risk. The profit-impact dimension distinguishes them. A good answer explicitly compares the axes rather than associating every “important-sounding” item with the strategic quadrant.

## Strategic purchases

Strategic items have both high profit impact and high supply risk. They require significant managerial attention, long-term planning, careful risk assessment and often close collaboration. The lecture's example is engines and its strategy is partnership.

For Campus Tees, a specialised printing system and its associated technical support could be strategic if they are central to the offer and difficult to source elsewhere. The relationship may require shared development, capacity discussions, performance monitoring, contingencies and senior management attention.

Partnership is a direction, not a guarantee of friendliness or equal interests. The supplier has its own customers, costs and priorities. The buyer needs to understand dependence and develop credible commitments. A contract can support the relationship, but the quality of interaction and the availability of alternatives remain important.

A high-impact/high-risk situation can sometimes be changed. Redesign may allow more standard components. Supplier development may create another source. A change in technology may make the purchase less unique. Strategic management includes changing the situation where feasible, not only accepting the quadrant.

## Kraljic's four-stage process

[diagram:kraljicprocess]

### Stage 1: purchasing classification

Assess categories using profit impact and supply risk. Determine which purchases deserve which form of attention. The familiar two-by-two matrix belongs here. Classification should be based on the business situation and reviewed when conditions change.

### Stage 2: market analysis

Examine the strength and characteristics of the supply market and the buying company. Relevant considerations include supplier capacity and utilisation, cost structures, break-even conditions, uniqueness, entry barriers, demand growth, the buyer's volume and the consequences of non-delivery. The article uses a structured comparison rather than a vague impression of whether a supplier seems powerful.

A buyer large in its own market can still be a small customer for a global supplier. Conversely, a supplier with a unique product may depend heavily on one customer's volume. Relative position must be assessed for the relationship and requirement under discussion.

### Stage 3: strategic positioning

For significant purchases, compare the company's buying strength with the supply market's strength. Kraljic uses a further three-by-three portfolio analysis with broad directions to **exploit**, **balance** or **diversify**. This is a separate analysis from the first two-by-two matrix.

Where buying strength is high relative to supply-market strength, the firm can exploit its purchasing position carefully. In more balanced situations, it needs an appropriately balanced strategy. Where the supply market is stronger and dependence is high, diversification and reducing vulnerability become important.

Diversify does not necessarily mean splitting every current order equally tomorrow. The article notes that short-term concentration can sometimes help secure supply while the firm develops longer-term alternatives, substitutions or backward integration. Timing and feasibility matter.

### Stage 4: action plans

Translate the analysis into practical choices about volume, price, contracts, spot purchasing, new suppliers, stock, make-or-buy decisions, value engineering and logistics. Assign responsibility, timing and contingencies. A completed matrix without actions has not completed the strategic work.

For Campus Tees, an action plan might standardise office purchases, tender standard blanks among qualified sources, hold a buffer of a hard-to-source spare part, and schedule joint maintenance and capacity reviews with a critical equipment partner. Different purchases produce different actions because the risk and economic consequences differ.

## What makes an assessment credible?

Use evidence rather than only labels. For supply risk, ask how many suppliers are technically qualified, how quickly they can deliver, whether capacity is constrained and what it takes to switch. For profit impact, ask about spend, final-product performance, growth opportunities and the economic effects of failure.

The original article emphasises organisational capabilities too: data, skills, management attention and forecasting. Purchasing needs enough information to anticipate problems rather than react to each missing item. Central coordination may create bargaining strength, while local knowledge can improve decisions about specific needs.

A rolling forecast is useful only if it connects to action. If a buyer sends forecasts nobody believes, the apparent information sharing may not improve supply. The quality of collaboration and the credibility of commitments influence how suppliers respond.

## Strengths and limitations

The matrix is easy to communicate, helps prioritise scarce management time and connects purchasing decisions with different supply conditions. It discourages one-size-fits-all purchasing. It also creates a common language for discussing why some purchases need collaboration and others need simpler processing.

Its categories can oversimplify a changing network. The boundaries between high and low depend on judgment. A supplier may provide several categories of item. Relationships can create capabilities not visible in a simple spend-risk score. One buyer's attempt to reduce risk can transfer it upstream rather than reduce it for the whole chain.

The model also needs regular reassessment. A disruption can move a standard blank shirt from leverage toward a higher-risk category. A redesign may move a unique connector out of the bottleneck quadrant. Dynamic analysis is stronger than memorising a permanent list of products.

Use the matrix together with the network perspective. Kraljic helps differentiate purchasing priorities. ARA helps explain the relationships, resources and activities through which a strategy is actually implemented. A strategic partnership only creates value if those connections work.

### Check your understanding

A €2 component has only one qualified source, but spending on it is tiny. Is it routine because it is cheap?

[answer:Possible answer]
No. Low spending does not imply low supply risk. If the component has low overall profit impact but high supply risk, it is a bottleneck item and continuity measures are appropriate. If its effect on business performance makes profit impact high as well, it may be strategic. Explain the chosen assessment rather than deciding from price alone.
