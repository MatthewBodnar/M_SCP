# Lean, agile and leagile supply chains

Lean and agile are responses to different operating conditions. Lean emphasises waste elimination and stable flow. Agile emphasises responsiveness in volatile markets. Naylor, Naim and Berry show how they can be combined in one supply chain through a decoupling point. The word **leagility** names that combination.

The central design problem is timing. Some activities take longer than the customer is willing to wait. A business must decide what to prepare in advance, what to leave until an actual order arrives and where to hold stock or capacity. The answer affects cost, variety, speed and risk.

## P-time, D-time and the gap

**P-time** is the production lead time; the lecture's diagram follows source, make and deliver. **D-time** is the time the customer is willing to wait. If P-time is longer than D-time, the full process cannot begin only after receiving the customer order and still meet the promise.

[diagram:ptime]

Suppose obtaining blanks takes five days, printing takes two and delivery takes one. P-time is eight days. If a club will wait three days, Campus Tees needs some preparation in advance or a change to the process. It might hold blank shirts, shorten supply lead time, reserve capacity or offer a different promise.

The P-time minus D-time gap indicates the part that must be anticipated or otherwise bridged. Forecasting is one way to prepare, but postponement can reduce the detail that must be forecast. It is easier to prepare common blank shirts than to predict every club's final logo in advance.

Do not assume that all production should be forecast-driven or that all activities can wait for a firm order. The design depends on actual lead times and customer expectations. A unique engineered product may require a long accepted wait. A standard retail product may need immediate availability.

## The customer order decoupling point

The lecture defines the **CODP**, or customer order decoupling point, as “the point where the product is linked to a specific order”.

**In plain English:** before this point, the business is preparing something generic or based on expected demand. After this point, the work is connected to a particular customer's actual order. The point divides different information requirements and operating priorities.

Campus Tees may stock plain shirts and wait for confirmed artwork before printing. Its generic shirts are not yet assigned to one club. Printing links them to the specific order. If it instead stocked finished club designs, the decoupling point would be further downstream and demand would need to be predicted in more specific detail.

The CODP is not simply whichever physical warehouse appears in a drawing. It has an information meaning: where does a product become committed to a particular order? A stock point often supports that transition, but the conceptual distinction is the order linkage.

## Postponement

> “Postponement is a business strategy which maximizes possible benefit and minimizes risk by delaying further investment into a product or service until the last possible moment”.

**Source:** Operations lecture, slide 10.

**In plain English:** keep options open by delaying a decision or transformation that makes the product more specific, until better information is available. The objective is not to deliver late; it is to delay commitment while still meeting the customer's deadline.

[diagram:postpone]

For Campus Tees, buying generic white blanks before the event and printing confirmed designs later postpones differentiation. It reduces the risk of guessing the wrong design. It does not eliminate the need to predict total blank demand or the required size range.

The reading discusses examples such as delaying national configuration of printers. A common base can be held until the destination's needs are known, then configured appropriately. Benetton's delayed dyeing is another helpful illustration of postponing a colour decision until better demand information is available.

Postponement requires a suitable product and process. If final customisation takes longer than D-time, it cannot simply be moved after the order without changing something else. Modular design, reliable common components, flexible capacity and good information can make postponement feasible.

## Exact definitions: lean and agile

> “Leanness means developing a value stream to eliminate all waste, including time, and to ensure a level schedule.”

> “Agility means using market knowledge and a virtual corporation to exploit profitable opportunities in a volatile market place.”

**Source:** Naylor, Naim and Berry (1999), p. 108.

**Lean in plain English:** remove unnecessary work and delay, and make the flow stable and efficient. **Agile in plain English:** understand changing demand and use a flexible network of capabilities to respond profitably. Agility is more than rushing; it requires knowledge and the ability to reconfigure work.

A virtual corporation in this discussion concerns coordinated capabilities across organisations. It does not merely mean an online shop. The business can combine resources in a network without owning every activity, which connects the reading with purchasing and industrial networks.

## Compare the paradigms carefully

| Dimension | Lean emphasis | Agile emphasis | Shared concern |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Demand conditions | A reasonably level and predictable schedule helps. | Volatile demand requires flexibility. | Understanding the market. |
| Main competitive emphasis | Cost and efficient flow. | Service and responsiveness. | Quality and lead-time performance. |
| Waste elimination | Essential to the paradigm. | Desirable, while responsiveness remains central. | Avoid resources that add no useful value. |
| Reconfiguration | Useful, but stability supports the flow. | Rapid reconfiguration is essential. | Match capabilities to the task. |
| Buffers | Reduce unnecessary inventory and delay. | Some inventory or capacity may support response. | Use the minimum reasonable protection for the situation. |

The paper does not argue that one paradigm universally replaces the other. Many foundations are shared: market knowledge, integration and lead-time reduction. The difference concerns which attributes become essential for the operating conditions and order-winning requirements.

Lean does not mean “no inventory under any circumstances”. Agile does not mean “ignore costs”. Both should be economically sensible. A buffer or reserve capacity may be justified when it enables a profitable response to uncertainty. The paper's minimum reasonable inventory idea is more useful than blindly pursuing a literal zero.

## Leagility: combine the two

[diagram:leagile]

The standard leagile arrangement is **lean upstream, agile downstream**, with a decoupling point between them. Upstream, common work is planned and levelled where possible. Downstream, the business responds to actual customer orders and variety. A suitable stock point protects the responsive part from some upstream uncertainty.

Campus Tees buys common blanks efficiently in advance and then uses flexible printing to fulfil confirmed club designs. It avoids both extremes: attempting to source every input after a late order, or printing every possible club design for stock. Commonality supports efficiency; delayed differentiation supports responsiveness.

The combined system still needs coordination. If upstream supply is unreliable, downstream agility may be overwhelmed. If downstream printing capacity is insufficient, a stock of blanks does not guarantee fast delivery. Leagility is a design of connected activities, not a slogan attached to one warehouse.

## Five decoupling-point positions

| Position | What waits for the order? | What is prepared in advance? | Main implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Buy to order | Procurement as well as making and delivery. | Very little product-specific stock. | High customisation can require a long customer wait. |
| Make to order | Manufacturing or transformation. | Raw materials or suitable inputs. | Less finished-stock risk, but production time remains after the order. |
| Assemble to order | Final assembly or configuration. | Common components or modules. | Faster response with fewer finished variants in stock. |
| Make to stock | Mainly allocation and delivery of finished goods. | Finished products based on forecasts. | Quick service but greater exposure to variant forecast errors. |
| Ship to stock | The customer selects from locally positioned stock. | Finished goods near the market. | Very short wait with more downstream stock commitments. |

Moving the point downstream usually reduces the customer's remaining wait while increasing advance commitment to specific products or locations. Moving it upstream preserves flexibility and reduces some finished-stock exposure while asking the customer to wait longer. The precise result depends on the product, process and logistics design.

An assemble-to-order computer is easy to picture: common parts are stocked, then configured when the order arrives. A bespoke machine bought and built to order asks for a longer wait. A supermarket item has already been made and positioned in stock. Use familiar examples to make the timing trade-off clear.

## The generic strategy matrix

[diagram:generic]

The operations lecture compares demand predictability with supply lead time. With predictable demand and long lead times, lean planning and optimisation are emphasised. With predictable demand and short lead times, Kanban-style continuous replenishment is suitable. With unpredictable demand and long supply lead times, a hybrid using postponement can help. With unpredictable demand and short lead times, agile quick response is emphasised.

These are design directions, not automatic answers for every item. A product may have predictable base demand and unpredictable peaks. A supplier lead time may be short on average but unreliable. Good analysis uses the actual pattern rather than forcing every situation into a box based on one adjective.

## Push, pull and information

Push means releasing work based on a plan or forecast. Pull links replenishment or production to actual consumption or a downstream signal. A Kanban signal can authorise replenishment, but the system may still use forecasts for capacity and strategic planning. Pull does not mean the business never plans.

Similarly, a decoupling point does not eliminate all upstream information about final demand. Better visibility can improve upstream decisions even where common materials are replenished before a specific order. The issue is the level of commitment and the timing of the decision, not whether actors are allowed to share information.

## Order winners, qualifiers and the reading's cases

An order qualifier is a condition needed to be considered by the customer. An order winner differentiates the chosen offer. Adequate quality might be a qualifier while reliable rapid delivery wins the club's order. Over time, a former winner may become an expected minimum.

The reading's conceptual value relationship combines quality and service against cost and lead time. Treat it as a way to think about trade-offs, not a universal formula into which unrelated measurement units can be inserted to produce a precise score.

The historical computer case discusses reductions in manufacturing and information lead times and the consequences of an assemble-to-order position. It includes a manufacturing lead-time change from about 280 to 150 days and information lead time from about 45 to 8 days. The lesson is that material and information lead times both matter, especially with short product life cycles.

The paper's forecast-error illustrations show uncertainty growing as the forecast horizon extends. Their percentages belong to the historical illustration; do not present them as a general law for every industry. The general managerial implication is to avoid committing to unnecessary product detail before useful demand information becomes available.
