# One business, the whole module

This fictional case brings the topics together. It is designed to help you apply the models rather than remember each chapter as an unrelated list. Read the situation, pause to make a recommendation, then compare it with the reasoning below.

## The festival order

A student festival asks Campus Tees for 300 shirts in three weeks. It needs a clear volunteer identity, an attractive design and delivery before the event. Its budget is limited. Final size information will arrive next week, and the logo may still change. Campus Tees has a printing machine, a small warehouse and relationships with two wholesalers.

Wholesaler A is cheaper but sometimes late. Wholesaler B is more dependable and offers better stock information. A specialised printer spare has only one qualified source. The company is also considering outsourcing printing because an external specialist quotes a low unit price.

A good answer should not jump immediately to one tool. Begin with the customer's requirements and the business situation, then use models to examine the connected decisions. The purpose of a framework is to improve the recommendation, not to display every term regardless of relevance.

## Start with marketing and strategy

The club's need is identification and belonging at the event. Its want is a particular shirt design. Demand exists when the agreed specification is backed by an approved budget. This distinction matters because enthusiastic interest without a final budget is not yet a firm production requirement.

The market is organisational: the festival buys shirts to run an event. Demand for blanks and ink is derived from that downstream requirement. Campus Tees should understand the event's timing rather than interpret the purchase as an isolated order for cotton goods.

If Campus Tees targets clubs and positions itself around reliable event delivery, the date becomes central to the offer. A lower purchasing price that makes delivery much less reliable may conflict with the positioning. The target segment and promised benefit should guide the supply design.

The strategy questions are: where is the firm now, where does it want to be and how will it get there? Current constraints include limited capacity, uncertain information and supply risk. The desired outcome is a complete, correct order before the event at an acceptable total cost. The route combines specification control, purchasing, scheduling, postponement and contingency planning.

A resource-based analysis asks what supports the promise. The printer is useful but may be purchasable by rivals. A reliable combination of club knowledge, supplier relationships and scheduling routines might be more difficult to copy. Apply VRIN conditionally rather than declaring the company advantaged because it owns equipment.

## Map the chain and the network

Material flows from upstream manufacturers through wholesalers to Campus Tees and then to the club. Requirements information moves upstream. Availability and shipment information moves in both directions. The club judges the final outcome even if the immediate cause of failure originates several relationships away.

Using ARA, actor bonds include trust between Campus Tees and the wholesalers. Resource ties include the fit between blank-shirt specifications, stock data and the printing method. Activity links include order cutoffs, dispatch, printing and delivery. A change in the final size list affects all three layers.

The direct relationship with Wholesaler A is one analytical level. A's connection to its manufacturer belongs to the recognised small world if Campus Tees understands that dependency. A wider disruption in material supply can affect the relationship even when the focal firm does not see every upstream actor.

This explains why a new contract clause alone may not solve the delivery problem. If the actual constraint is upstream capacity, pressuring the wholesaler for a promise may create an unrealistic commitment. Networking should investigate and change the substance of the activity and resource connections.

## Choose purchasing approaches

The standard blanks might be leverage purchases if several qualified suppliers exist and spending is significant. Competitive comparison is appropriate, but the qualification must include quality and delivery, not only price. Wholesaler B's higher price may be justified by lower total risk and better information.

The specialised spare could be a bottleneck item if its direct profit impact is low but supply risk is high. Campus Tees should investigate a reasonable buffer, maintenance arrangements and alternative qualification. If the spare's business impact is assessed as high because it is central to output, the case may instead justify a strategic classification. State the assessment rather than force the object into a fixed category.

Office supplies are likely routine: simplify their administration. A critical printing platform or specialist capability may need a strategic relationship. This is the Kraljic principle in action: different situations need different managerial attention.

The outsourcing quote should be evaluated against total costs and future capability. Consider artwork transfer, quality checks, urgent changes, transport, coordination and dependence. Also ask whether outsourcing would weaken the knowledge needed to manage the customer promise. The lowest unit quote is not automatically the best make-or-buy decision.

Supplier-base structure offers choices. One qualified supplier can support depth and efficiency but increases dependence. Two qualified sources can provide options but require effort. Parallel sourcing across product lines may combine relationship depth and broader access to capabilities. A second supplier only helps if it can meet the actual requirement in time.

## Design the order fulfilment process

Campus Tees should make the order's information dependencies visible. Artwork approval, size confirmation, blank availability, printing capacity and carrier pickup all need dates and responsibilities. A process map can reveal waiting hidden between those tasks.

If artwork waits in a manager's inbox for two days, buying a faster printer may not solve the lead-time problem. A time-based map can separate useful activity from waiting. A clearer approval process might remove more elapsed time than a small increase in print speed.

The seven wastes provide investigation prompts. Overproduction would be printing speculative club logos. Defects would be an incorrect design. Inventory would include unnecessary finished variants. Motion and transport might result from a poor layout. Overprocessing might be repeated data entry. Waiting could arise from unclear approval. Each proposed improvement should address a real cause.

Quality responsibility should be built into the flow. The artwork agreed with the customer must match the production file. Blank quality must support the print method. Operators need clear standards. A final inspection cannot economically repair every failure created upstream.

## Use postponement and leagility

Final artwork and size information are uncertain, but the event date is fixed. Campus Tees can prepare common resources before the information is complete and delay irreversible customisation. Generic blanks can be stocked or reserved; logos should wait for approval.

This is postponement when it preserves the ability to deliver on time. The CODP occurs where a generic blank becomes linked to the specific order through allocation and printing. Lean upstream replenishment supports the common input, while agile downstream work handles confirmed design and mix.

The firm should compare P-time with D-time. If sourcing plus printing plus delivery takes eight days and the final order allows only three, some upstream work must be prepared in advance or the process must change. A manager cannot solve the mismatch by simply declaring the business “make to order”.

Capacity matters as well as inventory. Enough blanks do not help if the only machine is unavailable. Maintenance, setup planning and reserve capacity can support responsiveness and resilience. The right buffer may be a combination of material, time, information and capacity.

## Apply the calculations

Suppose standard annual blank demand is 12,000, order cost is €30 and annual holding cost is €2 per blank. EOQ gives 600. With 300 working days, daily demand is 40. A five-day lead time and safety stock of 60 produce a reorder point of 260.

These are planning baselines for ordinary demand. A one-off festival order may temporarily change the demand pattern. Campus Tees must incorporate the confirmed event requirement rather than assume the average sawtooth automatically protects every peak. The assumptions behind a calculation matter as much as the arithmetic.

If the event's price is €15 per shirt, variable cost €9 and allocated fixed cost €1,200, break-even is 200 shirts. At 300, the simple model produces €600 profit. A rushed delivery fee, reprint or outsourcing coordination cost would reduce that profit. Use the model to understand the economics, then include relevant changes.

ABC analysis may identify blank shirts as a high-value category deserving close control. The small printer spare may have low annual usage value but high supply risk. This shows why ABC and Kraljic answer different questions and why a low-value item can still require a continuity plan.

## Negotiate a workable package

Campus Tees wants an attractive price, timely delivery and flexibility. The wholesaler wants predictable volume, manageable changes and efficient dispatch. The parties can explore a forecast, reservation of common blanks, a final size deadline and staged delivery rather than bargaining only over cents per unit.

Preparation includes a credible alternative and a worst acceptable outcome. Greeting and introduction create a shared understanding of the event. Argumentation explains constraints. Compromise combines issues. Closure records specifications and dates. Realisation means both sides send information and deliver as promised.

A written deal should specify what happens if the club changes the design after approval. Without that rule, an integrative package can unravel under late changes. Clear responsibilities protect both the relationship and the customer promise.

## Respond to a disruption

Two days before printing, Wholesaler A reports that half the medium shirts will be late. An effective response uses the four Rs. Responsiveness concerns revising the plan quickly. Reliability concerns obtaining a realistic revised commitment and accurate information. Resilience concerns activating qualified alternatives or an agreed fallback. Relationships concern working with suppliers and the club to make the solution feasible.

Campus Tees should not silently substitute unsuitable sizes or quality. It should identify feasible options and agree any change with the customer. An honest partial solution may be better than an impossible full promise, but the event's actual needs determine what is acceptable.

After delivery, investigate the cause. Was the forecast late, stock data wrong, upstream capacity unavailable or the commitment unrealistic? Use the lesson to change relationships, replenishment rules or customer deadlines. This is continuous improvement and business networking, not merely a one-time emergency.

## What a strong integrated answer does

It identifies the customer's requirement, explains dependencies, selects relevant tools and makes a justified recommendation. It shows assumptions and trade-offs. It does not say “use lean, agile, Kraljic and ERP” without explaining what each changes.

For this case, a defensible recommendation could be: secure common inputs from qualified sources, postpone final printing until approval, schedule the full information and material flow, protect critical equipment continuity, negotiate a package that reduces both parties' uncertainty and monitor the final delivery outcome. Each clause links a concept to an action and a reason.
