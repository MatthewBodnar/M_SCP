# Purchasing, outsourcing and the supplier base

Purchasing influences far more than the price on an invoice. It determines access to goods, services, knowledge and capabilities used by the business. Gadde and Håkansson argue that purchasing becomes strategically important as firms specialise and depend more heavily on external resources.

The central decisions concern **the scope of supply**, **the structure of the supplier base** and **the nature of supplier relationships**. These decisions interact. Outsourcing a complex process can increase the importance of a close relationship; reducing the number of suppliers can change both learning opportunities and supply risk.

## The source definition of purchasing

> “The management of the company’s external resources in such a way that the supply of all goods, services, capabilities and knowledge which are necessary for running, maintaining and managing the company’s primary and support activities is secured at the most favourable conditions.”

**Source:** Purchasing lecture, slide 3, citing van Weele (2005:12).

**In plain English:** obtain and manage the outside resources the whole business needs, under conditions that work well for the business. “Most favourable” should not be reduced to the lowest unit price. Quality, delivery, knowledge, risk, flexibility and the total relationship can matter.

The same slide gives a simpler formulation, attributed to Teigen in Axelsson: purchasing concerns activities that render an incoming bill. That is a memorable practical description, but the fuller definition makes its strategic scope clearer. Buying software, a maintenance service or specialist design knowledge can be purchasing just as buying blank shirts is.

## Why purchasing can have a large financial effect

The lecture's DuPont illustration uses turnover of 100, purchases of 60, additional costs of 38 and assets of 20. Profit is 2. Profit margin is 2/100 = 2%. Asset turnover is 100/20 = 5. Return on investment is margin multiplied by asset turnover: 2% × 5 = 10%.

If purchase spending falls by 5% of 60, it falls by 3 to 57. Assuming turnover, other costs and assets remain unchanged, profit rises to 5. Margin becomes 5%; asset turnover remains 5; ROI becomes 25%. Profit has increased by 150%, and ROI has risen by 15 percentage points.

| Measure | Before | After | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Turnover | 100 | 100 | Held constant in the illustration. |
| Purchases | 60 | 57 | A 5% reduction in purchasing spending. |
| Other costs | 38 | 38 | Held constant. |
| Profit | 2 | 5 | The saving feeds through to profit. |
| Assets | 20 | 20 | Held constant. |
| Profit margin | 2% | 5% | Profit divided by turnover. |
| Asset turnover | 5 | 5 | Turnover divided by assets. |
| ROI | 10% | 25% | Margin multiplied by asset turnover. |

The example demonstrates leverage, not a guarantee. A cheaper supplier could cause defects, late deliveries or larger inventory. Those effects would violate the illustration's assumptions. The strategic task is to improve the real economics of supply, rather than report a saving that reappears as a cost in another department.

## Make or buy: choose the scope of supply

The make-or-buy question asks which activities and resources should remain inside the firm and which should be obtained externally. It is not answered by comparing an internal wage with a supplier's invoice alone. Consider capability, knowledge, coordination, risk, capacity, quality and long-term strategic consequences.

Campus Tees could print internally, outsource printing completely, or combine internal routine work with external specialist work. If complex printing knowledge is central to its customer promise, outsourcing all difficult jobs could gradually weaken the capability it needs to compete. If a specialist can perform a non-core process much better, external sourcing may strengthen the overall offer.

The lecture and reading discuss a continuum from classical market contracting to quasi-vertical integration to full vertical integration. At one end, independent firms transact through market contracts. At the other, ownership places activities under the same organisational boundary. Between them, independent firms can coordinate closely through adaptations, shared tools, financial support or long-term commitments.

Quasi-integration explains why ownership and operational integration are different. A company can own less while coordinating more closely with suppliers. Conversely, ownership alone does not guarantee smooth coordination between departments or plants. The relevant issue is how resources and activities are actually connected.

### Core competence

The lecture draws on Prahalad and Hamel's core competence idea, describing it as a harmonised combination of multiple resources and skills that distinguishes a firm. Three tests are highlighted: potential access to a wide variety of markets, a significant contribution to customers' perceived benefits, and difficulty for competitors to imitate.

**In plain English:** a core competence is a deeply useful combination of abilities that helps the company compete across opportunities. It is not simply the product sold today or any task managers prefer to keep in-house.

For Campus Tees, understanding event requirements and coordinating reliable custom delivery may be more central than owning every transport vehicle. However, deciding what is core requires analysis. “We have always done this ourselves” is not one of the tests. Nor should a manager outsource a capability without considering whether it supports future learning and customer benefits.

## Outsourcing and its hidden costs

> “Complete transfer of a business process to an independent external organization”.

**Source:** Purchasing lecture, slide 7.

**In plain English:** another independent organisation takes responsibility for performing a process that the focal firm otherwise might carry out internally. The lecture notes that the assets are no longer maintained in-house. The buying firm still needs to manage the relationship and its consequences.

Outsourcing is different from offshoring. Outsourcing concerns who performs the work; offshoring concerns where it is performed. A local independent printer can be an outsourcing partner. A company's own foreign factory can be offshore without being outsourced.

The lecture lists motives such as cost cutting, focusing on core business, solving capability issues, improving service, accessing intellectual capital and managing or changing the business environment. Its historical survey charts illustrate those motives and reported difficulties; their percentages should not be presented as current universal facts.

The hidden-cost slide identifies quality costs, supplier/vendor relationship management, internal coordination and implementation of the external sourcing model. Campus Tees may pay less per printed shirt but spend more time transferring artwork, checking output, resolving misunderstandings and handling urgent customer changes.

| Possible gain | Related cost or risk | Question to ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lower processing price | More defects or rework. | What is the cost per acceptable delivered order? |
| Access to specialist equipment | Dependence on another firm's capacity choices. | What happens during a demand peak or breakdown? |
| Focus on core activities | Erosion of knowledge about the outsourced process. | Which capabilities must remain to evaluate and improve supply? |
| Less internal asset investment | More relationship-specific commitments. | How easy is a future change of partner? |
| Faster access to knowledge | Information-transfer and coordination work. | Who owns the interface and checks understanding? |

A balanced answer presents benefits and conditions. Outsourcing is neither automatically efficient nor automatically a loss of control. It changes the form of control, coordination and dependency. A successful arrangement needs a capable partner and a workable connection to internal activities.

## Supplier-base structure

Supplier-base structure concerns more than the number of suppliers. Gadde and Håkansson discuss how the supplier base is organised and how resources and responsibilities are distributed. Reducing supplier numbers can simplify some interfaces while making the remaining relationships more significant.

**Single sourcing** concentrates a purchase with one supplier in the relevant context. It can support scale, learning and close adaptation, but creates dependence and exposure to that supplier's disruption. **Multiple sourcing** uses more than one supplier for the same requirement, creating alternatives while adding qualification, coordination and relationship costs.

**Parallel sourcing** uses different suppliers for different models, plants or product lines while retaining relatively concentrated relationships within each. It can combine depth of collaboration with access to alternative capabilities across the portfolio. Do not define it as simply buying half of every order from two interchangeable vendors.

For Campus Tees, one supplier could handle standard cotton blanks and another the sports range. Within each product line, the relationship can be deep. Across the business, it retains knowledge of more than one supplier. Whether those suppliers can substitute for one another in an emergency still requires qualification; a second name on a list is not a functioning backup.

## Tiered supply and system suppliers

[diagram:tiers]

A tier-one supplier deals directly with the focal customer. Tier-two suppliers supply that supplier, and so on. A system supplier may deliver an integrated assembly or solution rather than an isolated component, coordinating lower-tier inputs and contributing development knowledge.

The reading's automotive examples show why a supplier's responsibility can expand. A firm buying a complete door system needs the system supplier to coordinate components, quality and delivery. Fewer direct interfaces do not mean the underlying network has become simple. Some coordination work has moved to another actor.

A T-shirt example would be a supplier delivering a complete event pack: shirt, print, individual label and packing. Campus Tees then has fewer direct purchasing interfaces, but depends on the supplier's ability to coordinate its own network. The buying firm still needs enough understanding to evaluate performance and manage strategic dependencies.

## What close relationships can achieve

The reading discusses rationalisation and technical development. Rationalisation can reduce administrative work, align production and improve material flows. Repeated paperwork may be replaced by shared information, consolidated invoicing or more predictable routines. The reading includes a reduction from many separate documents to a much smaller set as an illustration.

Joint production planning can reduce unnecessary buffers. Adapted packaging can reduce handling. Shared quality work can prevent repeated inspection and rework. These benefits arise through changes in the combined system, not simply through negotiating a lower transfer price.

Technical development involves combining knowledge and involving suppliers in development. A supplier may know how a material behaves; the buyer may know the final customer's application. Early collaboration can make design choices more feasible and reduce development time. It can also require commitments and disclosure that firms are initially reluctant to make.

Close relationships need investment. Adapted equipment, specialised knowledge and routines may have less value outside the relationship. That creates dependence and the possibility of opportunistic behaviour. Trust and credible commitment help, but they develop through experience and cannot be created by using the word “partnership” in a contract.

## JIT purchasing is more than small frequent deliveries

If a buyer demands daily deliveries while its supplier builds large buffers to cope with unstable schedules, inventory may merely have moved upstream. Whole-chain performance has not necessarily improved. Genuine coordination requires reliable quality, predictable information, appropriate transport and changes within the buyer as well as the supplier.

Campus Tees cannot expect its wholesaler to offer perfect responsiveness if it changes specifications after dispatch and refuses to share any demand information. The relationship must allow both parties to plan and solve problems. Long-term thinking does not remove disagreement; it gives a reason to address the total cost of the interface.

## Purchasing's organisational role

As purchases become more technical and strategically important, purchasing needs contact with design, operations, logistics, finance and sales. A central purchasing unit may aggregate volume and develop expertise. Decentralised decision making may preserve local technical knowledge and responsiveness. The question is how to combine coordination with useful knowledge.

Gadde and Håkansson's three strategic issues should therefore be discussed together. Make-or-buy changes which knowledge stays inside the firm. Supplier-base structure changes access to external capabilities. Relationship involvement changes how those capabilities can be combined. A narrow price-only approach misses their interaction.
