# Industrial networks: actors, resources and activities

The sample five-point question asks for the three parts of the networks model. Your first response should therefore be unmistakable: **actors, resources and activities**, connected through **actor bonds, resource ties and activity links**. The ARA model helps explain what a business relationship contains and how relationships connect into a wider network.

A relationship is more than two firms exchanging an invoice. They learn about each other, adapt equipment or knowledge, coordinate work and develop expectations. Those changes can create value and reduce uncertainty, but they can also create dependence and limit future choices.

## The ARA model

[diagram:ara]

| Layer | What it concerns | Relationship connection | Campus Tees example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Actors | Firms, people and their identities, intentions and knowledge of others. | Actor bonds. | The printer and supplier trust each other's commitments. |
| Resources | Physical and intangible things used and combined in business. | Resource ties. | Shirt specifications and ordering data are adapted to work together. |
| Activities | The things actors do and how their work is coordinated. | Activity links. | The supplier's dispatch timetable fits the printer's production sequence. |

Use **who**, **what** and **doing** as a memory aid. Actors are who is involved. Resources are what is used. Activities are what is done. The formal model concerns their connectedness; the memory aid is not an exact definition from the paper.

An actor bond can include trust, commitment, mutual knowledge and expectations. Resource ties arise when resources are combined or adapted across firms. Activity links connect tasks across organisational boundaries. A shared file is not automatically a meaningful resource tie unless it actually affects how the firms use resources together.

The layers interact. Trust may make the supplier willing to reserve capacity. Reserved capacity changes the resources available to Campus Tees. The firms then coordinate deliveries differently. If the delivery arrangement succeeds repeatedly, actor bonds can strengthen. You should explain at least one such connection in a five-point answer rather than treating the three parts as unrelated boxes.

## Why interaction matters

Ford and Mouzas study networking as a response to business problems. Firms do not begin with full knowledge of every relevant actor and resource. They learn through interaction, and their actions trigger responses. Relationships develop through connected episodes, not through one independent decision that settles everything.

A request for a different shirt fabric may require the wholesaler to contact a manufacturer. The manufacturer may suggest a different print process, which changes Campus Tees' work and its promise to the club. The original problem travels through connected relationships. No single firm fully controls all of those consequences.

Interaction includes substantive changes to activities and resources. It is not only communication or attendance at business events. A phone call can be part of networking, but the analytical question is what the actors are trying to change in their relationships and what changes follow.

## The IMP interaction model

[diagram:interaction]

The lecture's interaction model places exchanges and relationship development within several surrounding conditions. At the participant level, consider organisations' technology, structure and strategy, and individuals' aims, experience and characteristics. At the episode level, consider product or service exchange, information exchange, financial exchange and social exchange.

Over time, repeated exchanges can become institutionalised: the parties develop established expectations and routines. They can also adapt to one another. Campus Tees might learn the supplier's order cutoff and use a common size-code format. The supplier might change packing so the printing team can process batches more easily.

The **atmosphere** includes power and dependence, cooperation, closeness and expectations. The wider **environment** includes market structure, dynamism, internationalisation, position in the production or marketing channel and the social system. These conditions influence interaction; they do not mechanically determine one inevitable outcome.

An exam example could show a technical problem handled differently under different atmospheres. In a cooperative relationship, the parties might investigate it jointly. In a hostile relationship, they may withhold information and blame one another. The physical defect is the same, but the relationship changes the response and its cost.

Interaction can reduce uncertainty because firms learn what others can do. It can simultaneously create dependence because routines and resources become adapted to a particular partner. This dual effect is central to a balanced discussion of close relationships.

## Business networking: the exact definition

> “Business networking is the conscious problem-driven attempts of one or more business actors to change or develop some aspect(s) of the substance of interaction in relationships in which they and others are involved.”

**Source:** Ford and Mouzas (2013), p. 436.

**In plain English:** one or more actors deliberately try to change how business relationships work because they face a problem. They may change activities, resources, expectations or their interaction with other connected actors.

For Campus Tees, a problem could be repeated late deliveries. Networking might involve agreeing a new cutoff with the wholesaler, changing the carrier handover and persuading clubs to approve artwork earlier. The solution is not contained within a single contract clause or a single firm. It concerns the substance of connected relationships.

Networking does not require harmony. Actors may disagree, negotiate, compete or attempt to influence others. A supplier's effort to obtain a higher price and a buyer's effort to reduce delivery risk can both be networking attempts. Their goals may partly overlap and partly conflict.

## Three layers and three levels: keep them separate

The ARA layers are **activities, resources and actors**. Ford and Mouzas also distinguish **relationship, small world and wider world** as levels of network analysis. These are different dimensions. The sample question about three parts of the networks model should not be answered only with the three levels.

[diagram:networklevels]

A **relationship** level focuses on the direct dyad, such as Campus Tees and its wholesaler. The **small world** includes connected relationships recognised by the actors, such as the wholesaler's relationship with a manufacturer or Campus Tees' relationship with a major club. The **wider world** includes more distant changes and actors beyond that recognised immediate network.

Across all three levels, you can examine activities, resources and actors. This creates a three-by-three analytical view. A direct delivery problem concerns activity links in a relationship. A change in a manufacturer's fabric technology concerns resources in the small world. A new industry regulation may concern the wider world but eventually alter the direct relationship.

The paper links the activity layer with interdependence, the resource layer with heterogeneity and the actor layer with jointness. Over time, these can be considered through specialisation, path dependence and co-evolution. The point is that networks develop through connected histories rather than through independent interchangeable transactions.

| Perspective | Activities | Resources | Actors |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Direct relationship | How two firms coordinate work. | How their resources fit or are adapted. | How they understand and depend on one another. |
| Small world | How a change affects connected tasks. | How nearby resource combinations create possibilities. | How known third parties influence the relationship. |
| Wider world | Broader changes in business activity. | New technologies or resource constraints. | Distant actors, institutions and competitive changes. |

This table is a teaching interpretation of the paper's layered analysis. Use it to reason about a case; do not assume that memorising nine short cell labels alone demonstrates understanding.

## Network pictures are partial

A network picture is an actor's understanding of the surrounding network. It is subjective and incomplete. Campus Tees may think a delivery problem is caused by the wholesaler's warehouse. The wholesaler may see a manufacturer's allocation decision. The club may see only an unreliable printer.

Different pictures affect action. If a manager misunderstands the source of a constraint, they may pressure the wrong partner. Interaction can improve understanding, but no actor suddenly obtains a perfect view of the whole network. This explains why adaptation and learning continue over time.

The network view also challenges simple ownership boundaries. A resource's value can depend on how it combines with resources controlled by other firms. A machine may be much more useful when a supplier provides specialised material and a customer offers reliable demand information. What the focal firm owns is only part of the explanation.

## Three networking paradoxes

**Relationships both enable and constrain.** A close supplier can provide knowledge and reliability, but relationship-specific adaptations can make switching difficult. The actor faces choices about conforming to an existing arrangement or confronting it to change something.

**Actors shape relationships and are shaped by them.** Campus Tees can try to develop a relationship, yet the partner's routines and expectations also influence Campus Tees' behaviour. There is a tension between consolidating established relationships and creating new ones.

**Actors seek control but cannot control the entire network.** A buyer can exert pressure, but partners have other relationships and their own goals. There is a tension between coercing others and conceding to them. An attempt to control one issue may generate an unexpected response elsewhere.

A good discussion gives both sides of each paradox. “Relationships are useful” is incomplete. “Dependence is bad” is also incomplete. The point is that beneficial coordination and constraints can arise together.

## The detergent case in Ford and Mouzas

The paper follows P&G's response to problems in the German detergent market and its interactions with retailer Tengelmann. It includes competition with Henkel, market information from research organisations, product development and connections with P&G's wider organisation. The case is historical; it is not a claim about current company arrangements.

A more environmentally acceptable product and changing retail relationships cannot be understood as a product decision made in isolation. Resources and knowledge had to be combined across organisations. Successful action could influence other retailers, while competitors and retailers responded according to their own situations.

The paper also discusses Aldi's private-label requirements and differing supplier responses. The example shows that an opportunity attractive to one actor can conflict with another actor's existing strategy or relationships. A manufacturer's decision is shaped by its network position, not merely the size of a possible order.

German packaging requirements created wider-world pressures that affected costs and activities within direct relationships. Category management involved changes in how purchasing and selling activities were connected. This is a useful example of a general problem being addressed through particular relationships and of changes crossing the actor, resource and activity layers.

You do not need to reproduce every company detail unless asked about the case. The transferable lesson is that business problems and their solutions develop through connected relationships, with consequences that are neither fully predictable nor controlled by one participant.

## Indirect networking and managerial implications

A firm can influence a situation through another actor rather than by building a direct relationship with everyone. An intermediary may already possess knowledge and connections that make coordination more efficient. Campus Tees might work through its wholesaler rather than negotiating separately with every upstream mill.

Standardisation and customisation create another tension. Common routines can reduce cost, while tailored adaptations can create value for a particular relationship. Too much standardisation may ignore a customer's specific problem. Too much customisation may create complexity and dependence. Effective networking involves choosing what to adapt, with whom and for what purpose.
