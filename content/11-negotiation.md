# Negotiation: reach a workable joint decision

Negotiation is needed when parties must reach a joint decision and have partly different preferences. They depend on one another enough to talk, but they do not begin with complete agreement. A price negotiation, a delivery-date discussion and a conversation about quality responsibilities can all involve negotiation.

The course's practical perspective emphasises understanding the other party's needs while communicating your own. A settlement that one side cannot or will not implement is weak, even if the meeting ends with a signature. This connects negotiation with long-term relationships and the realisation phase after agreement.

## Distributive and integrative negotiation

**Distributive negotiation** treats the relevant value as a fixed pie: more for one party means less for the other. Price can have this structure if all other conditions are fixed. If the buyer pays €1 less, the seller receives €1 less.

**Integrative negotiation** looks for ways to create value by understanding different priorities and combining issues. It is often described as win-win. It is not simply being generous or splitting the difference. The parties search for arrangements that serve their needs better than a one-issue compromise would.

[diagram:negotiationpie]

Campus Tees wants a lower price and reliable supply. The supplier wants predictable volume and fewer last-minute changes. A package with a credible volume commitment, earlier information and staged deliveries may reduce the supplier's cost while improving the buyer's price and continuity. The gain comes from changing the arrangement, not from pretending the parties have identical interests.

A real negotiation can contain both value creation and value distribution. The parties might jointly find an efficient delivery plan and still disagree about how to share the saving. Calling a process integrative does not remove bargaining or the need to protect important interests.

Distributive behaviour is not necessarily dishonest, and integrative language is not proof of fairness. Analyse what issues are being negotiated, what alternatives exist and whether the arrangement is feasible. The course's advice explicitly includes not lying.

## Prepare before bargaining

Preparation means collecting information, understanding needs, ranking priorities, setting objectives and considering the other party's situation. It also means knowing what you will do if no agreement is reached. Entering a meeting without an alternative makes it easier to accept an unsuitable proposal.

The lecture distinguishes three objective levels: the worst acceptable outcome, a fair or realistic outcome, and the best outcome you hope to obtain. These help you evaluate offers without improvising every judgment under pressure. Be clear about which conditions are essential and which can be exchanged.

Campus Tees could prepare a target price, a maximum acceptable total cost, required quality, a latest delivery date and a credible forecast of volume. It should also know whether an alternative supplier is actually qualified. A quoted alternative that cannot meet the deadline is not equivalent to a functioning fallback.

Research the partner's constraints. A supplier might resist lower prices because setup costs are high, but accept a different batch pattern. Asking why a condition matters can reveal possibilities that a sequence of demands would miss. Understanding is useful even when the parties eventually decide not to agree.

## BATNA and the reservation threshold

**BATNA** means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement, associated with Fisher and Ury. It is what you can do if the negotiation does not produce an acceptable deal. A reservation threshold is the point beyond which the proposed agreement is worse than the acceptable alternative, after relevant differences are considered.

These are not identical concepts. “Use Supplier B” is an alternative course of action. “Do not accept a total adjusted cost above €1,650” is a decision threshold. The threshold may be derived from the alternative's price, quality, lead time and risk.

Suppose Supplier B can deliver the required blanks for €1,600 with acceptable quality and a feasible schedule. Supplier A's €1,550 offer may still be worse if it creates €200 of extra handling or unacceptable delivery risk. Compare complete arrangements rather than one headline number.

Do not bluff about an alternative you do not have. The lecture advises honesty. A credible BATNA strengthens the ability to decline a poor agreement; it need not be used as a threat. Sometimes the rational outcome is no agreement, particularly if every available package would leave a party worse off than its alternative.

## Seven phases of the negotiation process

[diagram:phases]

| Phase | Purpose | Practical Campus Tees example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Preparations | Gather information, needs, priorities, objectives and alternatives. | Calculate feasible volumes, dates and fallback options. |
| Greeting | Establish contact and a workable atmosphere. | Meet respectfully and allow appropriate introductory conversation. |
| Introduction | Build a shared understanding and agenda. | Clarify the event requirement and issues to resolve. |
| Argumentation | Explain positions and reasons; understand the other side. | Discuss why delivery reliability and predictable volume matter. |
| Compromise | Explore exchanges and packages. | Link price, volume, schedule and delivery frequency. |
| Closure | Confirm the agreement clearly. | Write the specification, dates, responsibilities and price. |
| Realisation | Implement and follow through. | Send forecasts, release orders and review actual delivery. |

The process is a guide, not a promise that every conversation follows seven cleanly separated steps. A misunderstanding during compromise may require more introduction or argumentation. The phases help ensure that important work is not forgotten.

### Greeting and introduction

A useful atmosphere supports communication. Time spent building rapport can have value, especially when the parties expect an ongoing relationship. What feels respectful or efficient can vary across people and cultural contexts.

Introduction establishes the purpose and agenda. Beginning with shared or less controversial issues can help create a foundation. It also clarifies whether the people present have the authority and information needed to decide. A meeting can fail because the actual decision maker was never involved.

### Argumentation

Explain reasons rather than only repeat positions. “We require Friday delivery because the festival opens Saturday morning” is more informative than “Friday is non-negotiable”. The reason helps the supplier evaluate possible methods and alternatives.

Listen actively. Ask questions that reveal underlying needs. Avoid assuming that the first stated position is the full explanation. A request for a higher minimum order may reflect setup economics, transport cost or planning uncertainty, each of which may be addressed differently.

### Compromise and packages

Consider combinations of issues. A lower price may be exchanged for more predictable volume. Faster delivery may require an earlier artwork deadline. A longer contract may justify supplier investment, while the buyer needs suitable performance safeguards.

A package should not hide an essential problem. If the supplier cannot physically meet the required quality, adding an attractive price does not solve the need. The purpose is to find feasible trades, not to conceal incompatibility behind a complex agreement.

### Closure and realisation

Closure should state what was agreed, by whom, when and under what conditions. Written confirmation reduces later misunderstanding. Define specifications, quantities, dates, responsibilities and how exceptions will be handled.

Realisation means doing the agreed work. If Campus Tees promised a forecast every Monday and fails to send it, the negotiated delivery arrangement may stop working. Review performance and learn from the result. Successful implementation also influences actor bonds and the next negotiation.

## Practical advice from the lecture

The lecture emphasises listening, avoiding unnecessary ultimatums, remaining calm, understanding the other side, writing agreements down and never lying. It also offers practical bidding advice, such as letting the other side make the first bid. Treat that as the lecture's guidance rather than a universal claim that no other approach can ever be appropriate.

“Never give more than necessary” does not mean refuse every concession. It means understand the value of what you exchange and link concessions to a purpose. A concession that costs little to you but matters greatly to the other party can support an integrative package.

A threatened deadline or emotional outburst can narrow the conversation before useful options have been explored. Calmness makes it easier to compare an offer with objectives and alternatives. It also helps preserve a relationship when agreement is difficult.

## Intercultural negotiation

The lecture contrasts Swedish and Chinese tendencies using cultural concepts. It describes Swedish negotiation as relatively structured, prepared and oriented toward formal contracts and time discipline. It discusses Chinese negotiation through relationship, respect and concepts such as **guanxi**, **mianzi** and **bao**.

Guanxi concerns relationships and connections. Mianzi concerns face, respect and social standing. Bao concerns reciprocity. These concepts can help a negotiator notice why trust, introductions, respectful disagreement or reciprocal actions may matter. They are not shortcuts for predicting every individual's behaviour.

The lecture uses Hofstede-style dimensions, including individualism, power distance and masculinity, to discuss broad tendencies. Its Swedish illustration has high individualism, low power distance and low masculinity; its Chinese illustration has more collectivism, higher power distance and higher masculinity. These are teaching comparisons, not descriptions of every person or organisation.

A thoughtful response avoids stereotypes. Ask how this particular partner prefers to work, who participates in decisions, how agreement is confirmed and what timing is feasible. An individual may differ substantially from a national average or a classroom illustration.

The lecture also raises whether adapting to the other culture is always beneficial, drawing on work including Brett and Okumura, Francis, Liu and colleagues, and Thompson. The message is not a simple universal rule to imitate the other party completely. Adaptation can be interpreted differently; curiosity, preparation and mutual understanding are safer than mechanical assumptions.

## A full integrative example

Campus Tees initially asks for a 10% price reduction. The supplier initially asks for a six-month commitment. A distributive discussion might settle on 5% without changing anything else. An integrative discussion investigates why each issue matters.

The supplier explains that uncertain order timing causes costly small runs and urgent shipments. Campus Tees explains that it cannot store six months of finished variants and fears being tied to unsuitable quality. They explore a framework agreement with a forecast, agreed release windows, staged deliveries and quality requirements.

The supplier gains predictability. Campus Tees avoids receiving everything at once and gets more dependable supply. The parties still negotiate the price and risk allocation, but the package creates possibilities beyond the original single-number argument. Realisation then tests whether forecasts, releases and quality actually work.
