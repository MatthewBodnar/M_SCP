## Match the command verb

**Describe** means explain what the model or concept contains and how it works. **Exemplify** means make it concrete with a fitting example. **Discuss** means examine implications, conditions, strengths, limitations or trade-offs. A ten-point answer that merely lists four boxes is unlikely to satisfy all three demands in the sample question.

A useful structure is definition, components, mechanism, example and evaluation. Scale the depth to the points and the time available. Keep the model's exact terms visible so the examiner can identify your knowledge. Do not bury the answer inside an unrelated story.

For calculations, show variables, formula, substitution, result with units and a short interpretation. If assumptions matter, state them. A correct number without an explanation can hide a misunderstanding; a clear method also makes an arithmetic slip easier to diagnose.

## Sample Q1: the network model — 5 points

**Prompt supplied by the teacher:** Describe the three different parts of the networks model presented in class as well as in the readings by Ford and Mouzas.

Before revealing the model, write the three parts from memory and give one connected example. Do not look at the chapter title while doing it.

[answer:Model answer · ARA]
The ARA network model describes business networks through actors, resources and activities. In relationships, these are connected through actor bonds, resource ties and activity links.

Actors are the firms and people involved. Actor bonds concern how they understand one another, their identities, trust, commitment and expectations. For example, a T-shirt printer and its wholesaler may develop confidence in each other's promises after repeated successful orders.

Resources are the physical and intangible means used in business, including equipment, materials, information and knowledge. Resource ties arise as firms combine or adapt resources. The printer's process may be adapted to a supplier's fabric specification, while shared stock information supports their joint work.

Activities are what actors do, such as purchasing, production, transport and administration. Activity links connect these tasks across firms. The wholesaler's dispatch schedule may be coordinated with the printer's production sequence and the customer's event deadline.

The three parts influence one another. Trust can encourage resource adaptation; adapted resources require coordinated activities; successful coordination can strengthen actor bonds. The model therefore explains a connected relationship rather than three isolated elements. Ford and Mouzas also examine relationship, small-world and wider-world levels, but these are analytical levels and should not replace the ARA parts in this answer.
[/answer]

**Why this works:** it names the parts, explains the matching connections, exemplifies each and shows interdependence. It avoids confusing the model with another three-part framework.

## Sample Q2: Kraljic — 10 points

**Prompt supplied by the teacher:** Describe, exemplify and discuss the Kraljic matrix used for industrial purchasing and supply management.

Try drawing the axes and placing the four quadrants before reading the answer. Label the high and low directions. Then add an example and strategy to each quadrant.

[answer:Model answer · Kraljic]
Kraljic's purchasing portfolio matrix differentiates supply-management approaches according to two dimensions: the purchase's profit impact and its supply risk. Profit impact concerns its significance for business results, including spend and effects on product performance. Supply risk concerns availability, qualified sources, substitution and uncertainty in the supply market. In the lecture, profit impact increases upward and supply risk to the right.

Routine or non-critical purchases have low profit impact and low supply risk. Office supplies are the lecture's example. The appropriate emphasis is rationalisation: simplify and standardise purchasing so administrative effort is proportionate to the purchase.

Leverage purchases have high profit impact and low supply risk. The lecture gives raw materials and semi-manufactured components as examples. With credible alternatives, the buyer can use competitive bidding, volume and comparison to improve the offer. Qualification and total cost remain important; a nominally cheap alternative may fail quality or delivery requirements.

Bottleneck purchases have low profit impact but high supply risk. The lecture gives flavours as an example and safety stock as a suggested response. The priority is continuity rather than a small price saving. Assurance arrangements, backup qualification or redesign may reduce vulnerability, subject to storage, shelf-life and technical constraints.

Strategic purchases have both high profit impact and high supply risk. Engines are the lecture's example. They require significant attention, long-term planning and often partnership, including information sharing, development and contingency arrangements. Partnership still involves different interests and dependence; it is not a guarantee that risk disappears.

The matrix helps allocate managerial attention and avoids treating every purchase in the same way. However, classification depends on context and can change. A standard material may become risky during a disruption; a redesign may create alternatives for a unique component. High and low boundaries require evidence and judgment, and the model can understate network connections if used mechanically.

Kraljic's article places the matrix within four stages: classification, market analysis, strategic positioning and action plans. Market analysis compares company and supply-market strength. A further portfolio analysis supports exploit, balance or diversify directions; it is separate from the initial two-by-two classification. The final result should be concrete actions concerning suppliers, contracts, stock, volume, make-or-buy and logistics. A strong application therefore combines the clear portfolio logic with total-cost analysis and understanding of the relationships needed to implement it.
[/answer]

**Why this works:** it answers all three command verbs, retains the lecture examples and strategies, and includes the wider article without confusing its second portfolio analysis with the basic matrix.

[answer:Answer map]

Distinguish logistics' movement, storage and related information from the broader interfirm relationship scope. ## Practice: Barney and VRIN

**Prompt:** Explain the resource-based view and discuss when a resource can support sustained competitive advantage.

[answer:Answer map]
Define resources broadly and name physical, human and organisational capital. State heterogeneity and imperfect mobility. Distinguish competitive advantage from sustained advantage in terms of competitors duplicating benefits. Explain valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable and non-substitutable. Develop history, causal ambiguity and social complexity with examples. Explain strategic substitutes and environmental change. A standard machine alone is usually easier to copy than an embedded organisational capability. Use the original VRIN framework rather than silently substituting VRIO.
[/answer]

## Practice: purchasing's three strategic issues

**Prompt:** Discuss the three strategic purchasing issues identified by Gadde and Håkansson.

[answer:Answer map]
Discuss scope/make-or-buy, supplier-base structure and relationship involvement. Explain how they interact: outsourcing complex work raises external dependence; supplier reduction can deepen collaboration while concentrating risk; adapted relationships can create rationalisation and development benefits. Add hidden costs, capability erosion, technical knowledge, tiered supply and internal cross-functional coordination. Avoid treating the issues as independent yes/no decisions.
[/answer]

## Practice: lean, agile and leagile

**Prompt:** Compare lean and agile and explain how a company can combine them.

[answer:Answer map]
Give the reading's definitions, then explain stable flow and waste elimination versus responsiveness in volatile markets. Name shared foundations: market knowledge, integration and lead-time reduction. Explain P-time, D-time and the CODP. Show lean upstream common work and agile downstream order-specific work. Use generic blanks and late printing or common printer configuration. Discuss inventory, capacity, product design and timing conditions. Do not equate lean with zero stock or agility with unlimited cost.
[/answer]

## Practice: EOQ and reorder point

**Prompt:** Annual demand is 12,000, ordering cost €30 and holding cost €2 per unit per year. Demand occurs over 300 working days. Lead time is five working days and safety stock is 60. Calculate EOQ and ROP and explain their different purposes.

[answer:Worked answer]
EOQ = √(2DK/h) = √(2 × 12,000 × 30 / 2) = 600 units. It answers how much to order. Daily demand = 12,000/300 = 40. ROP = dL + SS = 40 × 5 + 60 = 260 units. It answers when to trigger replenishment. At Q = 600, there are 20 orders annually, average cycle stock is 300, annual ordering cost is €600 and annual cycle-stock holding cost is €600. State the basic assumptions and keep daily and annual units consistent.
[/answer]

## Practice: quality and process improvement

**Prompt:** Explain how process mapping and quality approaches can reduce lead time and cost.

[answer:Answer map]
Start with the transformation process and the customer outcome. Separate activity time from waiting using a time-based map. Name and exemplify the seven wastes. Distinguish TQM, Kaizen, BPR and Six Sigma. Use a fishbone diagram to organise possible causes while stating that evidence is needed to confirm them. Link shorter flow and fewer defects to working capital, capacity, development and quality costs. Do not assume cutting inspection alone creates quality.
[/answer]

## Practice: negotiation

**Prompt:** Compare distributive and integrative negotiation and explain the importance of preparation and implementation.

[answer:Answer map]
Explain fixed-pie distribution versus value creation through differing priorities. Use a price/volume/delivery package. State BATNA as an alternative action and distinguish the reservation threshold. Name the seven phases and give particular attention to closure and realisation. Written terms and follow-through make the agreement work. Discuss cultural awareness without stereotyping individuals. A real negotiation can both create and divide value.
[/answer]

## Frequent mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Correction |
| --- | --- |
| ARA means relationship, small world and wider world. | Those are analysis levels; ARA means actors, resources and activities. |
| Kraljic classifies items only by price. | Use profit impact and supply risk. |
| Every expensive item is strategic. | High impact plus low risk can be leverage. |
| A cheap component is unimportant. | It may create high supply risk or stop operations. |
| Sustained advantage means a set number of years. | Barney focuses on rivals' inability to duplicate benefits. |
| Lean requires literal zero inventory. | Remove waste while maintaining an appropriate functioning system. |
| Agile simply means work faster. | It involves knowledge, flexibility and response in volatile markets. |
| Postponement means delayed customer delivery. | Delay commitment or differentiation while meeting the promise. |
| EOQ is the reorder point. | Quantity and timing are different decisions. |
| Larger EOQ lots require a unit-price discount. | The basic model works with constant price and fewer order events. |
| VMI automatically transfers stock ownership. | It specifies vendor monitoring and replenishment responsibility. |
| Integrative means split the difference. | Investigate priorities and create value through packages. |

## Manage your remaining study time

Allocate effort according to understanding, not only page count. Begin with the sample topics, then secure the connections and calculations. A ten-point question normally deserves more development than a five-point question, but the total exam duration has not been supplied, so this guide does not invent a fixed minutes-per-question rule.

Practise retrieving an answer before reopening it. Read a model, close it, explain it aloud and then check missing elements. If you cannot explain an example without the terminology, revisit the plain-English section. If you understand the example but cannot name the formal components, practise the exact labels.

Use uncertainty honestly. State assumptions in a case. If a classification depends on whether alternatives are qualified, explain both the condition and the recommendation. A justified answer demonstrates more understanding than an unsupported confident label.
