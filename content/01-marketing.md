# Marketing starts with a customer

Marketing is the bridge between what people need and what an organisation can deliver. Supply chain management is the work that makes that promise happen. A company can advertise a wonderful product, but if its suppliers cannot deliver, its factory makes mistakes, or its shop has no stock, the customer still experiences failure. This module therefore connects customers, strategy, business relationships, purchasing, operations, inventory and negotiation.

Our running example is **Campus Tees**, a small fictional business that prints T-shirts for student clubs. It buys blank shirts and ink, accepts club orders, prints designs and arranges delivery. The examples and numbers are teaching illustrations, not facts about a real company. Keep asking: what does the club value, what must the whole network do to provide it, and what trade-offs follow?

## Marketing: the source definition

> “A social and managerial process by which individuals and groups obtain what they need and want through creating and exchanging products and value with others.”

**Source:** Marketing lecture, slide 2.

**In plain English:** people have needs; organisations create something useful; the two sides exchange value. The customer usually gives money, but the important idea is an exchange that makes sense to both sides. Marketing includes understanding customers and designing the offer, not only advertising something after it has been made.

Campus Tees does not merely sell a cotton object. It helps a club look united at a festival, identify its volunteers and remember an event. The club may value a guaranteed delivery date more than a small price reduction. A good marketing decision starts with that benefit. It then guides purchasing, production and logistics: the chosen shirt, supplier, printing process and delivery service must support the promise.

A useful exam explanation connects the definition to behaviour. If you write only “marketing means selling”, you miss the process of understanding needs, creating value and exchanging it. Selling can be one activity within marketing. A business that sells aggressively but offers the wrong product has not solved the customer's problem.

## Needs, wants and demands

These terms describe different layers of the same customer situation. They are related, but they are not interchangeable.

| Term | Exact wording in the lecture | Plain explanation | Campus Tees example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Need | “A state of felt deprivation.” | Something important feels missing. | Volunteers need to be identifiable and feel part of a team. |
| Want | “The form that a human need takes as shaped by culture and individual personality.” | The particular solution someone would like. | The club wants matching purple shirts with a large logo. |
| Demand | “Human wants that are backed by purchasing power.” | The customer wants it and can afford to buy it. | The club has an approved budget for 300 shirts. |

**Source:** Marketing lecture, slides 3–5.

Needs can be physical, social or individual. Food, clothing and safety illustrate physical needs. Belonging illustrates a social need. Knowledge and self-expression illustrate individual needs. People may look for an object that satisfies the need or try to reduce the need itself. A person who feels hungry can buy a meal; a person who cannot afford a restaurant can choose a cheaper way to eat.

Do not assume a want automatically becomes market demand. Many students might want a very expensive customised jacket. That does not tell Campus Tees how many jackets will actually be ordered. Purchasing power, budget approval and willingness to pay matter. In a business market, there may also be a purchasing procedure before the money can be spent.

The flow from need to want to demand is useful for analysing a failed offer. Was the underlying need weak? Did the proposed product not match the desired form? Or did customers like it but lack purchasing power? Each explanation suggests a different managerial response. Advertising harder is not a universal solution.

## Industrial marketing and derived demand

> “Marketing of goods and services to individuals and organizations for purposes other than personal consumption”.

**Source:** Marketing lecture, slide 6.

**In plain English:** industrial marketing is selling things that another organisation uses to do its work, make something else or serve its own customers. The distinction concerns the purpose of the purchase. The same laptop can be a consumer purchase for home entertainment or a business purchase for a design company.

A wholesaler selling 300 blank shirts to Campus Tees is operating in a business market. Campus Tees uses those inputs to create an output. A student buying one finished shirt for personal use is a consumer. A student union buying shirts for its staff or event is an organisational buyer, even though people ultimately wear the garments.

**Derived demand** means demand for an input depends on demand further downstream. If the festival sells more tickets, its organiser needs more volunteers; more volunteers may require more shirts; Campus Tees then orders more blanks; the wholesaler orders more from the manufacturer. A change at the end can therefore affect many upstream businesses.

Derived demand explains why purchasing staff should understand the customer's market. A shirt supplier who only examines last month's purchase orders might miss an approaching festival peak. Information about final demand can make production and replenishment decisions better. It does not remove every uncertainty, but it gives the network a more useful starting point.

Business markets often involve fewer, more significant customers, a more deliberate buying process, technical requirements and continuing relationships. Several people may influence a decision: the user, the purchasing officer, a technical specialist and the person approving the budget. These are useful explanatory distinctions; do not turn them into an absolute claim that every business purchase is complicated or that consumers never have relationships with suppliers.

| Dimension | Consumer illustration | Industrial illustration | Managerial implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | A student buys a shirt to wear. | A printer buys blanks to fulfil orders. | Understand how the purchase supports another business activity. |
| Demand | Personal preference and income matter. | Downstream customer orders drive input needs. | Follow the end market, not only immediate orders. |
| Specification | Appearance and comfort dominate. | Printability, size consistency and delivery reliability also matter. | Translate customer benefits into operational requirements. |
| Relationship | A one-off purchase may be enough. | Repeated orders can justify shared routines. | Account for adaptation, trust and switching costs. |
| Decision | One person may decide quickly. | Several roles may approve the order. | Find out whose problem each feature solves. |

## Segmentation: divide the market usefully

> “To divide a market into distinct groups of buyers with different needs, characteristics or behaviors, who might require separate products or marketing mixes.”

**Source:** Marketing lecture, slide 8.

**In plain English:** customers are not all looking for the same thing. Put customers with similar needs together so that the offer can fit them better.

Campus Tees could distinguish student clubs, sports teams and local businesses. Clubs may prioritise low cost and a memorable event design. Sports teams may prioritise durability and repeated replacement orders. Businesses may prioritise accurate brand colours and consistent quality. A single undifferentiated offer could serve all three poorly.

A segment should be useful for action. Dividing customers by a characteristic that has no connection to their needs gives little help. If students with different hair colours buy in exactly the same way, hair colour is a weak basis for this business. A useful basis reveals different requirements, buying behaviour or economics.

Industrial segmentation can consider industry, size, location, application, purchasing approach or service requirements. These are explanatory examples of segmentation bases. The course's central definition is the idea of distinct buyer groups requiring different offers. In an exam, use an example that makes the difference in needs visible rather than simply naming categories.

## STP: segmentation, targeting and positioning

The lecture presents a sequence of six tasks. First identify segmentation bases and develop segment profiles. Next evaluate the attractiveness of each segment and select target segments. Finally develop positioning for each target segment and develop the relevant marketing mix.

[diagram:stp]

**Segmentation asks:** what different groups exist? **Targeting asks:** which groups should we serve? **Positioning asks:** how should those customers understand the distinctive benefit of our offer relative to alternatives?

Campus Tees might choose clubs ordering 100–500 shirts because its equipment handles those batches well. It could position itself around “reliable event shirts without complicated ordering”. That positioning should influence actual decisions: a clear order form, agreed artwork deadlines, dependable blanks and a delivery buffer. A slogan unsupported by the supply chain is a fragile promise.

The familiar product, price, place and promotion framework is a helpful way to unpack a marketing mix. Product concerns the offer and its service features; price concerns what the customer gives up; place concerns access and delivery; promotion concerns communication. This explanation helps connect the lecture's STP steps to practice. Do not assume a long discussion of the four Ps is required when the question asks specifically about industrial networks or purchasing.

Targeting also involves saying no to some opportunities. If Campus Tees accepts every urgent one-off job, it may disrupt the batches promised to its chosen club segment. The lost focus can increase setups, stock variety and late deliveries. Marketing strategy and operations strategy must therefore be compatible.

## Customer value is a whole-system result

Customers compare benefits with sacrifices. Benefits can include performance, confidence, convenience, service and speed. Sacrifices can include price, time, effort and risk. This is a simple explanation of value, not a substitute for the exact source definitions elsewhere in this guide.

Suppose Supplier A charges €4.80 per blank shirt but sends inconsistent sizes and arrives late. Supplier B charges €5.00 but delivers consistently and resolves defects quickly. The lower invoice price may not produce the lower total cost or the greater customer value. Reprinting, expedited transport, staff time and disappointed clubs all matter.

The module repeatedly returns to this distinction. Marketing identifies valued benefits. Strategy explains why the company can provide them better than rivals. Purchasing secures external resources. Operations transforms them. Inventory bridges mismatches in time. Logistics coordinates movement and storage. Networks and negotiation help organisations work together despite having different interests.

### Exam-ready takeaway

Begin with the exact term. Explain it in ordinary language. Show a realistic business situation. Then connect it to a managerial decision. For marketing, the critical connection is that customer benefits must guide the design and coordination of the supply chain.
