# Supply chains, logistics and integration

A supply chain is not simply a row of factories. It is a set of connected organisations whose decisions affect one another. Campus Tees depends on a blank-shirt supplier, a manufacturer, material suppliers, a delivery company, its own printing work and the club receiving the finished order. Each participant sees only part of the total situation, yet the customer's experience depends on their combined performance.

## The source definitions

> “The management of upstream and downstream relationships with suppliers and customers in order to deliver superior customer value at less cost to the supply chain as a whole.”

**Source:** SCM and Strategy lecture, slide 2; Christopher excerpt.

**In plain English:** coordinate the relationships on both sides of the company so that customers receive a better offer and the total system uses fewer resources. The definition contains two goals together: customer value and whole-chain cost. It also makes relationships central rather than treating the chain as only physical transport.

The Christopher excerpt also gives Aitken's definition of a supply chain:

> “A network of connected and interdependent organisations mutually and co-operatively working together to control, manage and improve the flow of materials and information from suppliers to end users.”

**In plain English:** different businesses depend on one another, and they need to manage both the things moving through the system and the knowledge needed to move them sensibly. A network is a more realistic picture than one straight line because firms normally have several suppliers and customers.

For logistics, Christopher writes:

> “Logistics is the process of strategically managing the procurement, movement and storage of materials, parts and finished inventory (and the related information flows) through the organisation and its marketing channels in such a way that current and future profitability are maximised through the cost-effective fulfilment of orders.”

**In plain English:** logistics gets the right materials and products through procurement, storage and delivery so that orders are fulfilled economically. It includes the information necessary to do this. It is not a synonym for driving a truck.

The supplied excerpt distinguishes logistics' integration of flows from supply chain management's broader coordination of relationships across organisations. The boundaries overlap in practice. For exam purposes, describe SCM as wider than a single firm's movement and storage activities, while keeping the customer-value and total-cost objective explicit.

## Follow the flows

[diagram:supply]

**Upstream** means toward the sources of supply. **Downstream** means toward the final customer. These are relative terms. The wholesaler is upstream of Campus Tees but downstream of the manufacturer. A company is not inherently “an upstream company” in every discussion; identify the focal firm and relationship.

Materials and products generally move downstream. Requirements information generally travels upstream: the club's order tells the printer what to make, and the printer's material order tells its supplier what is needed. Other information travels in both directions, such as availability, shipment status, forecasts and changes to specifications. Money usually moves upstream as customers pay suppliers. Returns can create reverse physical flows.

The lecture's logistics diagram emphasises the material flow and the requirements-information flow. The money and returns examples here broaden your understanding; they should not replace the specific arrows if a question asks you to reproduce that lecture model.

A simple example shows why information matters. If Campus Tees receives a final size list on Monday, it can order the correct blanks. If the club changes half the sizes on Thursday after printing begins, the same physical equipment now produces a less useful result. Better information can reduce unnecessary stock and rework. It cannot make an unavailable physical product instantly appear.

## The weakest link is judged by the customer

The lecture asks who decides the strength of a supply chain. The customer experiences the result. A printer can be excellent at printing while the club still receives its order late because a carrier misses the handover. Every firm may point to its own good performance measure, yet the end-to-end promise has failed.

The useful managerial response is to trace the dependency, not merely to blame the last visible firm. Did purchasing select an unreliable input source? Did sales promise an impossible date? Did operations finish after the carrier cutoff? Did the club supply artwork late? Supply chain management looks at how those activities fit together.

A chain metaphor makes dependency memorable, but the real structure is often a network. Campus Tees may have two blank suppliers, three couriers and several customers with conflicting deadlines. A change for one customer can affect resources used for another. This prepares you for the industrial-network view in the next chapters.

## Strategy's three questions and Ohmae's three Cs

The strategy lecture asks three practical questions: **Where are we? Where would we like to be? How do we get there?** An answer should connect the current position, a desired outcome and an actionable route. “We want to be better” is not enough because it does not specify what changes or why customers would care.

Ohmae's framework connects the **company, customers and competitors**. Customers seek benefits at acceptable prices. The company and competitors use assets and capabilities to provide those benefits, with differences in cost and effectiveness. A strategy needs a relative advantage: it must make sense against what alternatives can offer.

Campus Tees might discover that it is slower than a large online printer but better at helping local clubs finalise artwork. It could strengthen that service advantage while improving its own schedule. Copying the rival's lowest price without the rival's scale could undermine its economics. This illustrates the link between market understanding and resource choices.

## Porter's value chain

[diagram:valuechain]

The value chain breaks a firm's work into activities that contribute to value and cost. **Primary activities** are inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service. **Support activities** are firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology development and procurement. The lecture attributes this model to Michael Porter.

At Campus Tees, inbound logistics receives and checks blank shirts. Operations prints and finishes them. Outbound logistics packs and dispatches completed orders. Marketing and sales identifies suitable customers and agrees the offer. Service handles questions and resolves problems. Procurement selects and manages the external resources used across those activities.

Support does not mean unimportant. A poorly maintained order system can create errors across several primary activities. Good recruitment and training can improve quality and flexibility. Procurement can affect machines, ink, transport and knowledge, not only the first box of raw material entering the building.

Use the value chain to locate sources of advantage and connections between activities. If sales introduces unlimited design variants, operations may face more setups and purchasing may need more materials. A decision can add customer value while also creating cost elsewhere. The question is whether the combined outcome supports the strategy.

Do not confuse a firm's value chain with the entire supply network. The value chain is an analytical model of activities. Supply chain management also examines the connections between firms' activities. The two perspectives complement each other.

## Cost advantage and value advantage

[diagram:costvalue]

Christopher discusses competing through lower cost and through differentiated value. A cost advantage allows the firm to operate economically relative to competitors. A value advantage gives customers benefits they prefer, such as service, reliability or a tailored solution. High performance on both dimensions is desirable; there can be practical trade-offs in how it is achieved.

A commodity position offers little differentiation and little cost advantage. A service leader provides valued differences. A cost leader has a strong cost position. The strongest position combines a useful value proposition with an efficient system. Do not assume that value advantage simply means charging a higher price or that cost advantage means poor quality.

Campus Tees could reduce errors by simplifying its order process. That might simultaneously reduce administrative cost and improve the customer's experience. Conversely, offering every colour in immediate stock might improve availability while increasing inventory and obsolescence. You must analyse the mechanism rather than assuming every improvement helps every objective equally.

### Scale, learning and experience

Economies of scale can spread fixed costs across more output or allow more efficient resources. Learning can improve the way work is performed as people gain experience. An experience curve relates unit cost to **cumulative output**, not merely this week's production rate.

[diagram:experience]

If an employee learns how to position artwork correctly, printing errors can fall over time. If the firm buys a faster machine justified by a stable high volume, scale may reduce unit cost. These mechanisms are different. Producing unwanted shirts merely to increase volume can create more inventory cost and markdowns. Efficient production must still be connected to real demand.

## Four stages of supply chain integration

[diagram:integration]

The lecture presents a progression attributed to Stevens: baseline, functional integration, internal integration and external integration.

At the **baseline**, purchasing, material control, production, sales and distribution operate separately. Buffers and conflicting goals separate them. Purchasing may buy large lots to obtain a good price while production wants stable schedules and sales wants every variant immediately available.

**Functional integration** groups related work, such as materials management, manufacturing management and distribution. Coordination improves within those functions, but the whole company may still lack a single connected plan.

**Internal integration** connects the firm's functions so that purchasing, production and distribution work toward a shared outcome. A common plan helps prevent one department from making the others' work harder.

**External integration** extends coordination to suppliers and customers. Campus Tees may share expected event demand with a wholesaler and agree artwork deadlines with clubs. Integration is about coordinated decisions and information, not necessarily owning the other organisations.

The stages are a teaching model, not a promise that every organisation follows a smooth one-way path. A firm can be well integrated with one key supplier and poorly integrated internally elsewhere. Use the model to explain what changes in the scope of coordination.

## The four Rs of supply chain excellence

[diagram:fourrs]

| R | Meaning in the lecture | Simple example | Common confusion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Responsiveness | Time-based competition and agility. | Quickly revise a feasible printing plan after a size change. | Speed alone does not ensure consistent delivery. |
| Reliability | Reduce uncertainty and variability; improve visibility. | Deliver the agreed quantity on the agreed day repeatedly. | A fast average can hide very inconsistent outcomes. |
| Resilience | Deal with the unexpected and unplanned. | Use a qualified alternative when a printer breaks down. | Recovery capacity is different from normal efficiency. |
| Relationships | Manage dependencies as networks and outsourcing grow. | Maintain a supplier relationship that supports problem solving. | A friendly conversation is not enough without workable routines. |

The Rs reinforce one another but are distinct. Reliable information can support responsiveness. A strong relationship can help obtain emergency capacity. Resilience can require redundancy or spare capacity, which may raise ordinary operating cost. Discuss those trade-offs if asked to evaluate rather than only describe.

## Bullwhip: why orders become more unstable upstream

The three supplied past papers make clear that bullwhip deserves explicit revision. The **bullwhip effect** is the amplification of order variability as demand information moves upstream. A small variation in final-customer sales can lead to much larger variations in orders placed on wholesalers and manufacturers. An order contains replenishment decisions, not just a measurement of consumption.

**Easy example:** customers usually buy 100 shirts in a period and now buy 110. The retailer orders 130 to cover demand and adjust its inventory; the wholesaler orders 170 after making another forecast and buffer adjustment. These invented numbers illustrate the mechanism. There is no universal multiplier. A later correction can send orders sharply downward even when customer purchases remain relatively stable.

The primary research by Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang identifies demand-signal processing, order batching, price variation and rationing behaviour as causes. Independent forecasts can repeatedly reinterpret the signal. Large periodic batches create alternating peaks and gaps. Discounts encourage buying ahead of consumption. Buyers may exaggerate orders when they expect only part of each order to be allocated. Long lead times can amplify the forecast and buffer adjustments.

| Cause | Coordinated response | Why it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Repeated forecast and stock adjustment | Share actual sales, inventory and plans and agree replenishment rules. | Upstream firms can distinguish consumption from downstream stock corrections. |
| Batch ordering | Reduce avoidable order costs and coordinate feasible regular deliveries. | Orders more closely follow consumption instead of arriving in large spikes. |
| Promotional forward buying | Use more stable pricing and coordinate promotions. | Buying is less driven by temporary discounts. |
| Exaggerated orders during rationing | Use credible demand information and well-designed allocation rules. | Over-ordering becomes less attractive as a way to obtain stock. |

## Reducing risk through supply chain management

Supply chain risk is uncertainty threatening the chain's intended outcomes, such as delivery, quality and cost. A disruption can propagate across actors because production and customer promises depend on external resources. Identify the event, the exposed dependency and the consequence. Then select actions that reduce likelihood, impact or recovery time.

**Example one: a supplier stops operating.** A printer depending on one wholesaler cannot meet a club's deadline after that wholesaler's warehouse is damaged. Mapping dependencies, qualifying an alternative supplier with available capacity, agreeing recovery procedures and holding a suitable buffer can reduce interruption. Two apparent alternatives may share a factory, transport route or geographic hazard. Diversification must consider those common dependencies. Alternatives and inventory also cost money.

**Example two: demand is different from the forecast.** Printing every design before clubs confirm orders creates obsolete stock if preferences change. Shared order information, coordinated planning and postponement reduce premature commitment. Holding common blanks makes stock useful across designs. The printer still needs to forecast total demand and sizes and preserve sufficient downstream capacity to deliver after the order arrives.

These examples connect the four Rs to action: reliable information reduces avoidable variability; responsive processes support adjustment; resilience provides continuity and recovery; relationships enable interfirm problem solving. A useful answer discusses the cost and feasibility of each response rather than claiming all risk can be eliminated.

## Sustainability: two different problems, two different solutions

Sustainable SCM considers environmental and social outcomes alongside long-term economic viability across organisations. A whole-chain perspective asks whether a proposed improvement solves a problem or simply shifts it to a supplier, customer or another life-cycle stage. The examples below apply the module's coordination principles; they are teaching cases rather than empirical findings from the uploaded readings.

**Environmental problem: unsold finished shirts.** Overproduction consumes materials, energy and transport capacity without delivering the expected use. Pooling generic blanks, sharing confirmed demand and postponing design-specific printing can reduce obsolete finished inventory. Measure waste and obsolete units per fulfilled order and check whether urgent deliveries or small-batch setups offset some benefits. Postponement requires realistic printing capacity and deadlines.

**Social problem: unsafe working conditions upstream.** Supplier selection and management can assess conditions, develop corrective actions, obtain credible worker feedback and follow up improvements. Purchasing must also review its own behaviour: repeated unrealistic deadline changes may contribute to excessive overtime. Measure actual resolved problems and conditions, rather than treat a completed audit as proof of success. Long-term collaboration can support improvement, while serious unresolved failures may require escalation or alternative sourcing.

The first example addresses waste; the second addresses working conditions. Both need coordination across firms and workable economics. A ten-point answer should show two distinct problem–solution mechanisms, indicators and trade-offs.

## SCOR: a common language for supply chain processes

**SCOR** means **Supply Chain Operations Reference**. It provides common process definitions and performance measures to map, compare and improve supply chains. It is a framework for understanding work across organisational boundaries, not just a transport map or a piece of software.

In the classic vocabulary, **Plan** balances demand and resources; **Source** obtains inputs; **Make** transforms them; **Deliver** fulfils orders; **Return** manages reverse flows. Later classic versions, including SCOR 12, identify **Enable** for supporting management such as rules, data, contracts and risk. For Campus Tees this means planning club orders and capacity, buying blanks, printing, dispatching completed shirts and handling defective returns, with supporting information and management routines.

A manager might discover that long delivery lead time originates in waiting for artwork approval, rather than in transport. Process mapping and relevant measures help locate the problem and assess improvement without optimising one department at the expense of the chain.

**Version distinction:** ASCM's Digital Standard uses **Orchestrate** at level 0 and **Plan, Order, Source, Transform, Fulfill and Return** at level 1. The supplied course files do not establish which version the older exam expected. Learn the underlying purpose, label the version you describe, and use the teacher's terminology when specified. The ASCM primary references in Past exams show the distinction; do not silently mix old and new process lists.
