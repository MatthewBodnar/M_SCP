# Strategy and sustained competitive advantage

Why can one firm repeatedly perform better than another? An environmental explanation looks at industry conditions, customers and competitors. A resource-based explanation looks at differences in what firms control and how they can use it. Jay Barney's 1991 paper develops the second view without suggesting that the external environment becomes irrelevant.

Campus Tees and a rival may buy the same printer and the same shirts. If one consistently coordinates artwork, production and customer communication better, the difference may lie in its people, routines and relationships. The resource-based view asks whether such differences can create an advantage and whether competitors can duplicate the benefits.

## What counts as a resource?

Barney's definition states:

> “In this article, firm resources include all assets, capabilities, organizational processes, firm attributes, information, knowledge, etc. controlled by a firm that enable the firm to conceive of and implement strategies that improve its efficiency and effectiveness (Daft, 1983).”

**Source:** Barney (1991), p. 101; PDF page 3.

**In plain English:** a resource is something the firm controls that helps it think of and carry out a better way of competing. This can be a physical object, but it can also be knowledge, a process or a relationship. Owning a large building is not automatically strategically valuable; the resource must help the firm act effectively or efficiently in its situation.

Barney groups resources into physical capital, human capital and organisational capital. Physical capital includes technology, plant, equipment, location and access to raw materials. Human capital includes training, experience, judgment, intelligence, relationships and insight. Organisational capital includes reporting structures, planning and control systems, and informal relationships within and outside the firm.

A company vehicle is physical capital. An employee's ability to troubleshoot a printing fault is human capital. The routine that ensures artwork approval reaches production before a deadline is organisational capital. These resources often work together. A skilled employee without the right information may be ineffective; software without people who understand the business may simply automate mistakes.

The article notes that not every attribute of a firm necessarily qualifies as a useful resource in a particular analysis. Some attributes may prevent the firm from conceiving or implementing valuable strategies. Do not equate “something the company has” with “something that creates an advantage”.

## Two foundational assumptions

**Resource heterogeneity** means firms can control different bundles of strategically relevant resources. **Imperfect resource mobility** means those differences do not necessarily disappear immediately through trade or imitation. These assumptions allow persistent differences in performance to be explained.

Imagine that every printer could buy precisely the same machine, hire identical employees and copy every effective routine instantly. A resource-based explanation of a lasting advantage would be weak: whatever helped one firm could immediately help all the others. Barney instead examines conditions under which valuable differences can remain.

The view complements SWOT. Strengths and weaknesses concern the firm; opportunities and threats concern the environment. A resource is valuable because it helps deal with an opportunity or threat, not because a manager likes it. A superb routine for a product nobody wants may lose its strategic value after the market changes.

## Competitive advantage versus sustained advantage

Barney writes:

> “In this article, a firm is said to have a competitive advantage when it is implementing a value creating strategy not simultaneously being implemented by any current or potential competitors.”

He then defines sustained competitive advantage:

> “A firm is said to have a sustained competitive advantage when it is implementing a value creating strategy not simultaneously being implemented by any current or potential competitors and when these other firms are unable to duplicate the benefits of this strategy.”

**Source:** Barney (1991), p. 102; PDF page 4.

**In plain English:** an advantage means doing something value-creating that current and potential rivals are not doing at the same time. A sustained advantage adds a stronger condition: rivals cannot duplicate its benefits even after trying to do so.

“Sustained” is not defined as a fixed number of years. It does not mean that the advantage lasts forever. The definition concerns the outcome of competitive duplication efforts. A major environmental change can make previously useful resources less valuable. Barney discusses the possibility of fundamental, Schumpeterian changes in competitive conditions.

For Campus Tees, buying a new printing machine first might create a temporary advantage. If every competitor can buy the same machine next month, the equipment alone is unlikely to sustain it. A deeply developed combination of expertise, reputation and coordination might be harder to duplicate. Whether it actually qualifies requires analysis; the label is not a reward for being successful.

## VRIN: four tests

[diagram:vrin]

The original 1991 framework is **VRIN**: valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable and non-substitutable. Do not silently replace it with a later VRIO framework when the assigned paper is Barney (1991).

### Valuable

A resource is valuable when it enables strategies that exploit opportunities or neutralise threats. Campus Tees' reliable scheduling routine is valuable if clubs care about event deadlines and late delivery is a serious problem. If the market changes, the value of that same routine may change.

Ask “valuable for what, in which environment?” A technically impressive machine that cannot print the required material is not strategically useful just because it was expensive. A capability can become less valuable when customer needs or competing technologies shift.

### Rare

A valuable resource must also be sufficiently scarce among current and potential competitors to support advantage. If every printer offers the same reliable service, reliability may be essential for competing but not a source of superior performance. Common valuable resources can create **competitive parity**.

Rare does not mean that only one firm in the world possesses it. The question is whether enough competitors possess equivalent resources to make the same strategy commonplace. In an exam, explain the competitive implication rather than simply writing “not many firms have it”.

### Imperfectly imitable

Competitors must face difficulty obtaining or developing the resource. Barney identifies three main reasons: unique historical conditions, causal ambiguity and social complexity. The next section explains them separately because they are a frequent source of strong discussion marks.

### Non-substitutable

There must not be strategically equivalent alternative resources that are themselves common or readily imitable. A rival need not copy the exact resource if it can achieve the same benefits through a different route. “They cannot buy our exact machine” does not prove a sustained advantage if another technology produces an equivalent result easily.

Think about the whole strategy. A charismatic founder may inspire a common vision. Another organisation might achieve a comparable shared vision through a different management team or system. The substitute matters if it enables the competing strategy and is available under conditions that undermine the advantage.

## Three barriers to imitation

**Unique historical conditions and path dependence:** some resources exist because of a particular sequence of events. Campus Tees might have built a trusted relationship with several clubs through years of solving difficult event problems. A rival cannot simply buy the same shared history tomorrow. Historical origin alone is not enough, however; the resulting resource must still be valuable and rare.

**Causal ambiguity:** it may be unclear exactly why a firm succeeds. Is the result caused by hiring, routines, informal communication, a particular combination of them, or something else? If competitors cannot identify the causal mechanism, copying becomes difficult. The successful firm's own managers may also struggle to specify it completely.

**Social complexity:** resources can depend on complex relationships among people. Trust, culture, reputation and informal coordination may be hard to engineer through a simple purchase or instruction. A competitor can buy the same software but may not reproduce the relationships that make people share accurate information through it.

| Barrier | Everyday illustration | Why copying is difficult | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Historical conditions | A supplier trusts the firm after many successful joint projects. | The history cannot be instantly recreated. | Old relationships are not necessarily valuable forever. |
| Causal ambiguity | Several interacting practices seem to explain reliable delivery. | Rivals cannot isolate what to reproduce. | Uncertainty about causation is different from having no useful explanation. |
| Social complexity | Employees coordinate informally and solve problems together. | Interpersonal systems cannot be copied like a drawing. | “Good culture” needs a concrete mechanism in an answer. |

These mechanisms can overlap. A long history can create socially complex trust, and the combined routines may be causally ambiguous. Explain each clearly before discussing the interaction. Merely listing three technical terms demonstrates less understanding than showing what competitors would actually find hard to do.

## Substitution is not the same as imitation

Suppose Campus Tees relies on a highly skilled employee to check every order manually. A competitor develops a cheap, widely available order-validation system that achieves equivalent accuracy. The competitor has not copied the employee's personal knowledge. It may nevertheless duplicate the benefit of the strategy.

Barney discusses both similar and different resources as possible strategic substitutes. Another strong management team could substitute for a particular team. Different resources might support the same strategic purpose. The relevant question is whether an alternative allows competitors to implement a value-creating strategy with equivalent benefits.

This is why a VRIN answer needs all four tests. Value and rarity may explain an initial advantage. Imitation and substitution help explain whether its benefits can persist. Do not use “rare” and “hard to imitate” as synonyms: a rare item might be easy to copy once rivals notice it.

## Applications in the paper

Formal strategic planning can be widely available and codifiable, which limits its ability by itself to create sustained advantage. It may still help a firm identify and use other valuable resources. Informal or emergent planning may be more socially complex and harder to duplicate.

Information-processing equipment is another useful example. Standard computers and software can often be acquired by rivals. An information system embedded in a firm's managerial processes and relationships may be more difficult to reproduce. The distinction is between a purchasable tool and an effective organisational capability built around it.

Reputation can reflect unique history and complex social relationships. A competitor might offer a guarantee as an alternative way to reassure customers, but whether that is a strategically equivalent substitute requires analysis. A guarantee and a reputation do not automatically create the same customer confidence or cost structure.

The article also discusses managerial implications. Managers should identify, develop and protect useful resource combinations, while recognising that some sources of advantage cannot simply be bought in an open market. If every rival can acquire the same asset on identical terms, acquiring it alone is unlikely to explain a sustained difference.

## Connect strategy to the rest of the module

A supplier relationship can give access to knowledge and coordinated activities that are not contained in a single physical asset. A lean routine can create a cost advantage. An agile capability can create a service advantage in volatile conditions. A purchasing decision can protect or destroy access to valuable capabilities.

There is an important tension with the network perspective. Barney emphasises resources controlled by the firm. Ford and Mouzas emphasise how resource value depends on relationships and combinations across firms. You do not need to declare one perspective correct and the other wrong. Explain the different analytical focus: internal resource differences and external interdependence both help explain performance.
