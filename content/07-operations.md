# Operations: turn resources into outcomes

Operations management concerns the transformation of inputs into useful outputs. In a factory, the transformation may physically alter materials. In a service organisation, it may change information, a customer's location or a customer's condition. The central question is how resources and activities create the outcome the customer needs.

Campus Tees transforms blank shirts, ink, design information, labour, equipment and management effort into finished event shirts and associated service. A printing machine matters, but so do the processes that obtain correct artwork, allocate capacity and check whether an order is complete.

## The transformation model

[diagram:transformation]

The lecture identifies inputs such as material, machines, labour, management and capital. A transformation process uses them to create goods and services. Feedback connects the result to future decisions. If customers report that prints fade after washing, that information should influence materials, methods, quality checks or specifications.

**In plain English:** something comes in, work changes it, something useful comes out, and information about the result helps improve the next attempt. Do not assume “transformation” always means cutting or assembling a physical object. An accountant transforms records into financial information; a delivery company changes where a parcel is located.

Inputs can constrain the output in different ways. Excellent materials do not compensate for an impossible schedule. Skilled employees cannot use an unavailable machine. Capital invested in excessive inventory can limit funds available for improvements. The model encourages you to consider the combined system rather than one isolated resource.

## Processes, subprocesses and activities

A process connects a customer need to a satisfied customer through linked work. A subprocess is a part of that larger process. An activity is a more specific task. The level of detail depends on the question you are analysing.

For Campus Tees, “fulfil a club order” is a process. “Prepare approved artwork” can be a subprocess. “Check the file resolution” is an activity within it. Inputs and outputs connect the tasks: the approved file becomes an input to production planning and printing.

This distinction helps when diagnosing a problem. If late delivery originates in artwork approval, improving only the physical printing speed may have little effect. The process view follows the customer's outcome across departments and handovers. A departmental chart alone may hide the waiting between them.

A good map shows the actual process rather than an ideal procedure that staff do not follow. It should expose queues, repeated checks, missing information and unclear responsibilities. The purpose is to improve understanding and decisions, not to produce the most complicated possible diagram.

## Read the process-map symbols

The lecture's SCM symbols include an information flow, a material flow, a supplier or customer, delivery, inventory, a process step and a data box. It also distinguishes a push arrow and the movement of finished goods to a customer.

| Symbol or element | What it tells you | Campus Tees interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Information-flow line | A message or instruction moves. | An approved design or order enters planning. |
| Material-flow arrow | Physical things move. | Blank shirts move to printing. |
| Supplier/customer icon | An external party connects to the process. | A wholesaler supplies the blanks. |
| Delivery/truck | Transport or shipment occurs. | Finished shirts leave with the carrier. |
| Inventory triangle | Stock waits at a point in the flow. | A pile of blanks awaits processing. |
| Process box | Work transforms or handles something. | Printing, inspection or packing occurs. |
| Data box | Relevant measurements describe the step. | Cycle time, resources or other process data. |

Symbols help you see relationships consistently. They are not the analysis itself. A triangle tells you that stock exists; it does not tell you whether that stock is wasteful, necessary for service or compensating for unreliable supply. Ask why it is present and what would happen if it were removed.

## Quality approaches: TQM, Kaizen, BPR and Six Sigma

**Total Quality Management (TQM)** is described in the course as an organisation-wide effort to work with quality and continuous improvement. Quality is not left only to a final inspector. Purchasing, design, production, administration and customer service all affect it.

**Kaizen** means continuous improvement. Staff repeatedly make and evaluate improvements to the way work is done. Campus Tees might introduce a clearer artwork checklist, then refine it after identifying recurring misunderstandings. Small changes can accumulate into a substantial improvement.

**Business Process Reengineering (BPR)** concerns radical redesign of core workflows and processes for dramatic improvements in productivity, cycle time and quality. Instead of adding one more signature to an inefficient process, the firm may redesign how the order is handled from beginning to end.

**Six Sigma** is a data-driven approach to reducing defects and variability. In our example, the team would measure printing errors, investigate causes and assess whether changes reduce the problem. Do not use Six Sigma as a generic word for every improvement effort.

| Approach | Central emphasis | Simple illustration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| TQM | Organisation-wide responsibility for quality. | Sales, purchasing and printing jointly prevent incorrect orders. |
| Kaizen | Ongoing improvement. | Improve the order checklist every time a useful lesson appears. |
| BPR | Radical process redesign. | Replace several disconnected order forms with a redesigned end-to-end flow. |
| Six Sigma | Data, defects and variability. | Measure misprints and identify which conditions explain them. |

The glossary's statement that TQM is often replaced by ISO9000, Six Sigma and lean should be read as the source's broad teaching description. These ideas can coexist. Do not infer a universal rule that TQM has ceased to exist or that each later method has exactly the same purpose.

## The seven wastes: MUDA

The seven wastes in the lecture and glossary are overproduction, defects, inventory, motion, overprocessing, transporting and waiting. A practical answer defines each and shows what the unnecessary resource use looks like.

| Waste | Plain explanation | Campus Tees example | Improvement question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Overproduction | Make more or earlier than needed. | Print 100 unapproved extra shirts. | What real demand authorises this output? |
| Defects | Work fails requirements and needs correction or disposal. | Print the wrong logo. | How can the error be prevented at its source? |
| Inventory | Resources sit unused or waiting. | Keep boxes of unpopular sizes for months. | Which uncertainty or timing mismatch creates this stock? |
| Motion | Unnecessary movement by people. | Walk repeatedly across the room for tools. | Can the layout or workplace organisation improve? |
| Overprocessing | Do more work than the requirement needs. | Enter the same order details into three disconnected forms. | Does the customer or process need this extra step? |
| Transporting | Unnecessary movement of goods. | Carry batches between distant rooms several times. | Can material flow become shorter and simpler? |
| Waiting | People, machines or goods cannot proceed. | A finished batch waits for an approval already given by email. | What dependency or missing information causes the delay? |

Motion concerns people; transport concerns goods. Overproduction creates unnecessary output; inventory is the stock that may result. They are related but not identical. A stock of essential spare parts may be intentional protection rather than something to remove without analysis.

Waste elimination should preserve the required outcome. Removing a necessary quality check before the underlying process is capable can increase defects. The better question is how to prevent the defect and make the check unnecessary, not how to cut every visible activity immediately.

## Time-based process mapping

[diagram:timeprocess]

A time-based map separates activity time from waiting or wasted time. The lecture's example shows long delays between relatively short operations. Its redesigned process reduces elapsed time by addressing the way work is organised, rather than simply making every processing task faster.

In a simple teaching example, an order takes 16 hours from entry to completion. Four hours involve useful activity and twelve hours are waiting. If the activity is made 25% faster, one hour is saved. If half the waiting is removed, six hours are saved. This explains why managers should examine queues and handovers, not only machine speed.

The lecture's original map runs to roughly 69 elapsed hours and the redesigned version to roughly 38. These are visual readings of the chart, not precision measurements. The redesign includes booking work at goods-in, operators picking up their own jobs, multiskilling and self-inspection. The basic operations remain broadly similar while organisation changes substantially.

Waiting can arise from batching, unavailable information, separate inspection queues, unclear ownership or priorities that conflict across functions. Improvements should target the cause. Telling employees to “work faster” will not resolve a missing approval or a job waiting in the wrong queue.

## Cause-and-effect analysis

[diagram:fishbone]

A cause-and-effect or fishbone diagram organises possible causes of a problem. The lecture groups possibilities under people, machines, material and methods. These categories help teams investigate systematically rather than jump to the first plausible explanation.

For late orders, people-related possibilities might include missing training or unclear responsibility. Machine-related possibilities might include breakdowns or long setups. Material-related possibilities might include shortages or unsuitable blanks. Method-related possibilities might include duplicate data entry or batch rules.

The diagram contains hypotheses. It does not prove that every listed cause is real. Evidence, observation and measurement are needed. A good answer makes that distinction: brainstorming a possible explanation is the start of diagnosis, not the end.

A problem can involve several causes at once. A machine failure may be more damaging because the schedule has no recovery capacity and the supplier lead time for a spare is long. This connects operations with purchasing, inventory and resilience. The module's concepts become more useful when you combine them around a concrete problem.

## Why reducing waste and time can reduce cost

The operations lecture highlights working capital, plant and equipment capital, development costs and quality costs. Faster flow can reduce money tied up in work-in-progress. Better use of capacity can reduce the need for extra equipment. Shorter development cycles can reduce delays and repeated work. Preventing defects can reduce scrap, correction and customer-service costs.

These effects are conditional. A faster process that produces the wrong variant earlier has not necessarily improved value. A reduction in inventory that creates repeated stoppages may raise total cost. The aim is a better flow of useful output toward customer needs.

Why might a 50% faster machine produce only a small improvement in customer lead time?

[answer:Possible answer]
If most lead time consists of waiting for artwork, materials, inspection or dispatch, the machine affects only a small part of the total. A time-based process map identifies where the elapsed time is actually spent. Improvements to handovers and queues may save more time than increasing processing speed alone.
